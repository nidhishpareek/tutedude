Unit 02

Introduction to Cloud Computing

003 — What is Cloud Computing

  • A client computer is the machine in front of you. A server is a computer that stays on and answers other computers.
  • The internet is the network that connects them. The cloud is someone else's servers, reached over that network, that you rent instead of buying.
  • He draws the shift from one company owning every server in a room to using a provider's servers and paying for what is used.

Cloud computing. Using servers, storage, and software that a provider runs, reached over the internet, instead of hardware you own.

004 — Key Cloud Computing Concepts

  • On-demand means you start a resource when you need it and stop it when you do not.
  • Pay-as-you-go means the bill follows use, not a big purchase up front.
  • Scalability is adding or removing capacity. Elasticity is that change happening with demand, including shrinking again.
  • High availability means the service keeps answering even if one machine fails, usually by having more than one copy.

Elasticity. Capacity that grows and shrinks with demand.

High availability. The service stays reachable when a piece of it fails.

005 — Benefits of Cloud Computing

  • You stop buying and racking servers. Capacity is there when a launch or a sale needs it.
  • You can put copies of an app near users in more than one place, which cuts delay.
  • A provider's many customers share the cost of data centers, power, and staff.
  • You can try a design, throw it away, and start again without leftover hardware.

006 — Cloud Computing Deployment Model

  • Public cloud is a provider's data centers, shared by many customers, with your account walled off from theirs.
  • Private cloud is cloud-style tooling used by one organization, often in its own building.
  • Hybrid cloud keeps some systems on your own machines and some on a public cloud, and connects them.
  • He uses hybrid for data that must stay on site while the public app scales.

Public cloud. A shared provider cloud, isolated per customer account.

Hybrid cloud. Your own machines plus a public cloud, working together.

007 — Types of Cloud Computing

  • He maps three service shapes. IaaS gives you the machine and you manage the operating system up. PaaS gives you a place to run an app and hides the machine. SaaS gives you the finished app.
  • The line moves: more of the stack is the provider's job as you go from IaaS to SaaS, and you lose some control.
  • He will later pin EC2 to IaaS, Elastic Beanstalk to PaaS, and services like Gmail-style apps to SaaS.

IaaS. You rent the infrastructure and still manage the operating system and the app.

PaaS. You bring the app. The provider runs the platform under it.

SaaS. You use a finished application. The provider runs everything under it.

008 — History of Cloud Computing

  • He sketches the path from owning one server, to a virtual machine on that server, to renting virtual machines from a provider.
  • Amazon's own shopping peaks pushed it to build capacity it could also sell. That is the story he uses for why AWS exists.
  • Other providers followed. The idea that stuck is renting capacity by the hour instead of buying a room of servers.

009 — Cloud Computing Use Cases

  • A website that is quiet most of the year and slammed for a few days is the example he keeps using.
  • Companies move email, file storage, and backups off their own disks.
  • New products start small on the cloud and grow without a hardware order.
  • He also names disaster recovery: a second copy of the system in another place, ready if the first place fails.

010 — Cloud Shared Responsibility Model

  • The provider secures the building, the power, the hardware, and the network that connects its data centers.
  • You secure what you put on top: accounts, passwords, your data, and how you configure the service.
  • The split shifts by service type. On IaaS you patch the operating system. On SaaS you mostly manage users and data.

Shared responsibility model. The provider secures the cloud itself. You secure what you run and store in it.

011 — Advantages of Cloud Computing

  • He returns to speed, cost, and global reach, this time as exam-style advantages rather than a story.
  • Trade fixed capital cost for a variable bill. Stop guessing capacity months ahead.
  • Go global in minutes by picking another region, instead of building a second data center.
  • You still design for failure. The cloud does not make a single server magically safe.

012 — Disadvantages of Cloud Computing

  • A provider outage is your outage if you only run in one place.
  • The bill can surprise you if you leave resources running or pick the wrong storage class.
  • You depend on the provider's services and prices. Moving a large system out later is real work.
  • Data that cannot leave a country, or a building, may not fit a public region.

013 — Cloud Vs Traditional IT Infrastructure

  • Traditional IT is servers you buy, rack, power, and replace. Lead time is weeks. Capacity is whatever you bought.
  • Cloud IT is servers you rent. Lead time is minutes. Unused capacity can be stopped.
  • You still need people. They shift from fixing hardware to designing accounts, networks, and bills.
  • He treats this as the same idea as lecture 003, restated as a comparison you can answer on a test.

014 — Introduction to Cloud Computing Quiz

  • He walks sample items: what cloud computing is, what elasticity means, and which model is public, private, or hybrid.
  • IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS are separated by who manages the operating system and the app.
  • Shared responsibility: the building and hardware are the provider; data and access are yours.
  • He wants the definition first, then the option. A word like "patch the building" is never the customer's job.

015 — Pricing Advantages of Cloud Computing

  • No upfront hardware purchase. You pay for the hours and gigabytes you use.
  • Bigger volume can get a lower unit price, which he will later call volume discounts and consolidated billing.
  • Stopping a test server stops that part of the bill. A server in a closet does not work that way.
  • Reserved and spot options come later. Here he only wants the contrast with buying a box.

016 — Summary of Cloud Computing

  • He closes the section: cloud is rented IT, public or private or hybrid, shaped as IaaS, PaaS, or SaaS.
  • The advantages he wants remembered are elasticity, pay-as-you-go, and reach. The risks are outages, bills, and lock-in.
  • Shared responsibility is the frame for every later AWS service.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Defining cloud, the internet, and a server with a simple client-and-server drawing.
  • Comparing public, private, and hybrid, then IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS, then a short quiz.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Say what cloud computing is, and name elasticity, pay-as-you-go, and high availability.
  • Tell public from private from hybrid, and IaaS from PaaS from SaaS.
  • State which side of the shared responsibility model owns the building, and which side owns the data.

Unit 03

Cloud Computing Services

017 — Cloud Computing Services Overview

  • This unit names real providers and the kinds of products they sell, before he switches the rest of the course to AWS.
  • He will cover Google, Azure, IBM, and a few others, then a comparison and a quiz.
  • He does not open their consoles. The lecture is only so AWS is not the only provider name you know.

018 — Google Cloud

  • Google Cloud is Google's public cloud. He treats it as a peer of AWS, not a feature of AWS.
  • He points at compute, storage, and data products as the same three jobs every cloud sells.
  • You do not need the product names for the AWS exam. You need to know a cloud provider is a company, not a service inside AWS.

019 — IBM Cloud

  • IBM Cloud is another public cloud, aimed at companies that already run IBM software.
  • He uses it as one more example that "cloud" is a market, not a single vendor.
  • The shared idea is still renting compute and storage instead of buying them.

020 — Microsoft Azure

  • Azure is Microsoft's public cloud. He names it as the usual alternative when a shop is already on Windows and Microsoft 365.
  • The service shapes match: virtual machines, storage, and identity, under different product names.
  • For this course, Azure is a comparison point. The console he will click is AWS.

021 — Other Cloud Providers

  • He lists smaller or specialist clouds so AWS is not the only name in the market.
  • The pattern is the same: regions of data centers, an account, a bill based on use.
  • He does not configure any of them. This lecture is a map, not a lab.

022 — Cloud Computing Services Quiz

  • Questions ask which company is a cloud provider and which product is compute versus storage versus a finished app.
  • He knocks out options that mix a provider with an AWS service name.
  • The point of the quiz is vocabulary: provider, IaaS, PaaS, SaaS, public cloud.

023 — Cloud Service Provider

  • A cloud service provider is the company that owns the data centers and rents the services.
  • AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud are providers. EC2 and S3 are services from one provider.
  • You hold an account with the provider. The provider bills that account.

Cloud service provider. The company that runs the cloud and rents it to you.

024 — Google Workspace

  • Google Workspace is Gmail, Drive, and Docs sold as a finished suite. He files it under SaaS.
  • You do not patch a server to use Gmail. You manage users and the data you put in it.
  • He contrasts that with renting a virtual machine, where the operating system is still yours.

025 — Microsoft 365

  • Microsoft 365 is the same idea as Workspace: mail, files, and Office apps as SaaS.
  • Identity and files are the customer's problem. The servers under Outlook are Microsoft's.
  • He uses it so SaaS is a product you have seen, not only a definition.

026 — Cloud Services Comparison

  • He lines the providers up on compute, storage, and identity, and says the jobs match even when the names do not.
  • Picking a provider is about what you already run, where the data must live, and price, not about one cloud being "real."
  • From the next units on, every console click is AWS.

027 — Summary of Cloud Computing Services

  • Providers sell the same kinds of services. SaaS suites are the far end, where you only use the app.
  • AWS is the provider this course will stay inside.
  • He points forward to regions, accounts, and IAM.

028 — Cloud Computing Services Quiz

  • A second pass on provider versus service, and on which example is SaaS.
  • Workspace and Microsoft 365 are the SaaS examples. A virtual machine is not.
  • He wants you to eliminate answers that put hardware ownership on the customer inside a SaaS product.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Naming Google Cloud, Azure, IBM, and the big SaaS suites as examples, not as labs.
  • Quizzing provider versus service, and IaaS versus SaaS.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Name three cloud providers and say what a provider is.
  • Point at Workspace or Microsoft 365 and call it SaaS.
  • Keep AWS service names (EC2, S3) separate from the company that sells them.

Unit 04

Cloud Computing Models

029 — Cloud Computing Models Overview

  • He says this unit goes deeper on the three service models and the three deployment models, then ties them to shared responsibility.
  • The names are the ones from unit 02. The new work is who patches what.
  • The unit ends by moving the shared-responsibility line as the service model changes from IaaS to SaaS.

030 — IaaS (Infrastructure as a Service)

  • IaaS hands you compute, storage, and a network. A virtual machine is the picture he uses.
  • You install the operating system patches, the runtime, and the app. The provider keeps the hardware and the hypervisor running.
  • EC2 is the AWS example he flags, without opening it yet.

IaaS. Rented infrastructure. You still manage the operating system and everything above it.

031 — PaaS (Platform as a Service)

  • PaaS gives you a platform that already has the runtime. You upload the application.
  • You do not patch the operating system. You do own the app code and how it is configured.
  • He will later call Elastic Beanstalk the AWS example.

PaaS. A managed platform. You bring the application, not the server care.

032 — SaaS (Software as a Service)

  • SaaS is the finished product: mail, documents, a vendor's web app.
  • You manage users and the data you type in. You do not manage servers, patches, or the application code.
  • Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 are his examples from the previous unit.

SaaS. A finished application you subscribe to.

033 — Deployment Models

  • Public means the provider's cloud, many customers, your account isolated.
  • Private means one organization. Hybrid means both, connected.
  • He repeats that a deployment model is where it runs, and a service model is how much of the stack you manage. They are different questions.

034 — Shared Responsibility Model

  • He draws one stack and moves the line. In IaaS the line sits under the operating system. In PaaS it sits under the app. In SaaS it sits under the data.
  • Physical security of the data center is never the customer's job on AWS.
  • Customer work that stays in every model: turning on accounts carefully, and deciding who can see the data.

035 — Use Cases for Cloud Models

  • IaaS when you need a specific operating system or full control of the machine.
  • PaaS when a team wants to ship a web app and not run servers.
  • SaaS when the job is already a product, such as mail. Hybrid when part of the system must stay on machines you own.

036 — Summary of Cloud Computing Models

  • Service model answers "who manages the stack." Deployment model answers "whose building."
  • Shared responsibility follows the service model. The provider's share grows from IaaS to SaaS.
  • The next unit is where AWS actually places those buildings: regions, availability zones, and edge locations.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Separating IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS by who patches the operating system.
  • Pairing each with a deployment model and a shared-responsibility line.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Define IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS in one sentence each, with an example.
  • Define public, private, and hybrid without mixing them up with the service models.
  • Place the shared-responsibility line for each service model.

Unit 05

AWS Global Infrastructure

037 — AWS Global Infrastructure Overview

  • AWS is not one data center. He previews regions, availability zones, and edge locations, then a quiz.
  • The point of the unit is why an app is placed in more than one building.
  • He will use edge locations again when CloudFront caches content near users.

038 — AWS Global Infrastructure

  • A region is a group of data centers in one geographic area, named like us-east-1 or ap-south-1.
  • An availability zone is one or more data centers inside that region, with its own power and network, close enough for low delay and far enough that one fire does not take the others.
  • An edge location is a smaller site used to cache and speed up content near users. It is not a full region.
  • He tells you to pick a region for latency, law, and which services exist there, then use more than one zone so one zone can fail.

Region. A geographic area that holds several isolated data-center groups.

Availability zone. An isolated data-center group inside one region.

Edge location. A smaller AWS site that caches content close to users.

039 — AWS Global Infrastructure Quiz

  • A region is not one building, and it is not a content-delivery node. It is an area with multiple availability zones.
  • Edge locations are the part of the network that caches content near users. CloudFront is the service that uses them.
  • He rejects answers that put a virtual private cloud or a subnet in the place of global infrastructure.

040 — Benefits of Using AWS Global Infrastructure

  • High availability comes from running in more than one zone. Disaster recovery comes from a second region.
  • Latency drops when the region, or the edge cache, is near the user.
  • Some countries require data to stay in-country. Picking the region is how you meet that.
  • You pay for the extra copies. Global reach is a design choice, not an automatic feature of one server.

041 — Summary of AWS Global Infrastructure

  • Region, availability zone, edge location: three different sizes of place.
  • Put the app in at least two zones in the region you chose. Use a second region only when you need distance.
  • Edge locations come back when he teaches CloudFront.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Drawing a region that contains zones, and edge locations out near users.
  • Quizzing the three names against trap answers.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Define region, availability zone, and edge location.
  • Give one reason to pick a region and one reason to use two zones.
  • Say which of the three CloudFront uses to sit close to users.

Unit 06

AWS Account Creation and Security

042 — AWS Account Creation and Security Overview

  • He is about to make a real AWS account and then lock the root user down.
  • The unit is the console sign-up, a support plan, the root user, and multi-factor authentication.
  • The account has a credit card attached. Later labs can charge it if they are left running.

043 — Creating AWS Account

  • He signs up at the AWS site with an email address, a password, and a credit card. The email becomes the root user.
  • He picks a support plan. Basic support is free. Paid plans add people and faster response, which he returns to later.
  • After sign-in he lands in the console. The search bar at the top is how he will open every service.
  • He warns that the card can be charged. Turn things off, and do not leave experiments running.

044 — AWS Root User

  • The root user is the identity of the email that created the account. It can close the account, change billing, and do things no IAM user should do.
  • He says: sign in as root once, turn on MFA, make an administrator IAM user, and then stop using root for daily work.
  • Sharing the root password, or making access keys for root, is the mistake he wants you to avoid.

Root user. The account owner identity, created with the sign-up email. It is not an IAM user.

045 — Multi-Factor Authentication

  • A password is one factor. MFA adds a second, usually a code from an app on your phone.
  • He turns MFA on for the root user in the security credentials page: choose a virtual MFA device, scan the code, enter two consecutive codes.
  • He wants MFA on root first, then on any IAM user who can change the account.

Multi-factor authentication. A second proof, besides the password, before a sign-in is accepted.

046 — AWS Root User vs IAM User

  • Root owns the account. An IAM user is a person or app you create inside the account, with only the permissions you attach.
  • Daily console work, including creating EC2 and S3, should be an IAM user. Root is for the few account-level tasks.
  • He will build IAM users in the next unit. Here he only draws the line.

047 — AWS Account Creation and Security Quiz

  • Items ask which identity can close the account, and what MFA adds to a password.
  • Root is the answer for billing ownership and account closure. An IAM user is the answer for everyday admin.
  • He treats "turn on MFA and stop using root daily" as the security outcome of the unit.

048 — Summary of AWS Account Creation and Security

  • Create the account, know which email is root, turn on MFA, and plan to make an IAM admin.
  • Support plans exist. Basic is the free one. He will compare paid plans again in the billing unit.
  • Nothing in this unit launches a billable server. The next unit is IAM.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Creating an account, signing into the console, and pointing at the root identity.
  • Turning on a virtual MFA device for root and contrasting it with an IAM user.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Create an AWS account and find the console search bar.
  • Say what the root user is allowed to do that an IAM user should not.
  • Turn on MFA and explain why root should not be the daily login.

Unit 07

IAM (Identity and Access Management)

049 — IAM (Identity and Access Management) Overview

  • IAM is how AWS decides who can do what. He previews users, groups, roles, policies, and MFA.
  • The labs create a user, a group, a policy, and a role, then delete them.
  • The hands-on lectures create a user, a group, and a role, then delete them.

050 — IAM Quiz

  • He uses the quiz to plant definitions before the lectures: a user is a person, a group is a set of users, a role is assumed by a service or a person for a while.
  • A policy is the JSON document that allows or denies an action. A password is not a policy.
  • Root is not an IAM user. He keeps that distinction from the previous unit.

051 — IAM Introduction

  • IAM is global. Users you create are not stuck in one region, even though many resources are.
  • He opens the IAM console and shows users, user groups, roles, and policies in the left menu.
  • The default is deny. If no policy allows an action, the call fails.

IAM. The AWS service that creates identities and attaches permissions inside one account.

052 — IAM Users

  • An IAM user has a name and credentials for one person or one application. Console users get a password. Programs get access keys.
  • He creates a user, chooses console access, sets a custom password, and optionally forces a new password at first sign-in.
  • He does not attach full administrator access in the demo if he can avoid it. Least privilege is the rule he repeats.
  • Access keys are a second credential. He says not to make them unless a program needs them, and never for root.

IAM user. A long-lived identity inside the account, with its own password or keys.

053 — IAM User Groups

  • A group has no credentials of its own. You put users in it and attach policies to the group.
  • He makes a group, attaches a policy, and adds the user. Changing the group changes everyone in it.
  • A user can be in more than one group. Permissions add together.

IAM user group. A collection of users that share the policies attached to the group.

054 — IAM Policies

  • A policy is a JSON document with Effect (Allow or Deny), Action, and Resource.
  • AWS managed policies are ready-made, such as read-only or a service's full access. Customer managed policies are ones you write.
  • He shows an inline policy stuck to one user, and says a managed policy is easier to reuse.
  • An explicit Deny beats an Allow. Least privilege means the smallest Action list that still lets the job happen.

IAM policy. A JSON document that allows or denies actions on resources.

055 — IAM Roles

  • A role has no long-term password. A user, or an AWS service such as EC2, assumes it and gets temporary credentials.
  • He creates a role, picks a trusted service (EC2 is the example), attaches a policy, and names it.
  • An instance profile is how an EC2 instance receives that role. The instance then calls other services without keys sitting on the disk.

IAM role. An identity that is assumed for a session, often by an AWS service, with temporary credentials.

056 — IAM Best Practices

  • Lock root away, with MFA, and do not make root access keys.
  • Put people in groups. Attach policies to groups and roles, not a pile of inline policies on every user.
  • Prefer roles for applications on EC2, Lambda, and other services.
  • Turn on MFA for administrators. Review unused users and keys.

057 — IAM Shared Responsibility Model

  • AWS runs the IAM service: the hardware, the availability of the IAM API, and the global identity system.
  • You create users, write policies, turn on MFA, and rotate keys. A bad policy is your incident, not AWS's.
  • He uses this lecture as the pattern for later services: AWS runs the service, you configure how you use it.

058 — IAM Hands On

  • In the IAM console he creates a user with console access, a group, and attaches an AWS managed policy to the group.
  • He adds the user to the group and signs in as that user in a private window to show the smaller menu.
  • He then creates a role trusted by EC2 and attaches a small policy.
  • At the end he deletes the demo user, group, and role so the account stays clean.

059 — IAM Summary

  • Users are people. Groups bundle them. Policies are the permissions. Roles are temporary identities for services and cross-account work.
  • Least privilege and MFA are the two habits he wants from the unit.
  • Almost every later hands-on will ask for an IAM role. This is why.

060 — IAM Quiz

  • He answers items that mix up users, groups, and roles, and items where root is offered as the daily admin.
  • A policy is the object that allows an API action. A group cannot log in.
  • A role is the right choice when an EC2 instance must read an S3 bucket.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Creating a user, a group, a policy attachment, and a role in the IAM console, then deleting them.
  • Drawing least privilege, MFA, and the shared-responsibility split for IAM.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Create an IAM user and a group, and attach a managed policy to the group.
  • Read an Allow policy as Effect, Action, and Resource.
  • Explain when to use a role instead of an access key on a server.
  • State what AWS operates in IAM and what you must configure.

Unit 08

EC2 Fundamentals

061 — EC2 Fundamentals Overview

  • EC2 is the virtual server service. This unit is the first real compute lab: launch, connect, stop, terminate.
  • He will also name instance types, AMIs, key pairs, and security groups at a basic level.
  • The launch stays on the free tier: Amazon Linux and a micro instance, then he terminates it.

062 — EC2 Quiz

  • The warm-up quiz asks what EC2 is (virtual servers), and what you are billed for while an instance runs.
  • Stopping is not the same as terminating. He flags that distinction for the later lab.
  • A security group is the firewall of the instance, introduced here only as a name.

063 — EC2 Introduction

  • An EC2 instance is a virtual machine you rent by the second or hour. You pick the operating system, the size, the disk, and the network.
  • It is IaaS. You patch the guest operating system. AWS runs the physical host.
  • He opens the EC2 console and shows the instance list, which is empty until he launches one.

EC2. AWS virtual servers. You choose the image, size, disk, and network, and you manage the operating system.

064 — EC2 Instance Types

  • The family letter is the job. t is burstable and cheap, good for the labs. m is general. c is compute. r is memory. i is storage.
  • The size (micro, small, large) is how much CPU and memory that family member has.
  • He will launch t2.micro or t3.micro because those sit in the free tier. A larger size bills immediately.

Instance type. The size and specialty of an EC2 virtual machine, written like t2.micro.

065 — Launching EC2 Instance

  • Launch flow: name, Amazon Linux AMI, t2.micro or t3.micro, a key pair, a security group that allows SSH (port 22) from your IP, default VPC, and a small root disk.
  • He creates a key pair and downloads the .pem file once. Without that file you cannot SSH in later.
  • He starts the instance and waits until the state is running and a public IP appears.
  • Free tier is not unlimited. He says to terminate what you do not need.

066 — Connecting to EC2 Instance

  • From a Mac or Linux terminal he sets the key file's permissions so only the owner can read it, then uses ssh with the public IP and the ec2-user name for Amazon Linux.
  • Windows users can use a local SSH client the same way. He is not using a password. The key is the credential.
  • Once in, he runs a couple of commands to prove it is a Linux machine, then leaves the session.
  • If SSH hangs, the usual miss is the security group or the key name.

067 — EC2 Instance States

  • Pending means it is starting. Running means it is on and billing compute. Stopping moves it to stopped, where compute billing pauses and the disk remains.

Elastic Block Store (EBS)

084 — EBS Overview

  • EBS is the disk attached to EC2. This unit is volume types, snapshots, and what happens to the disk when the instance dies.
  • He contrasts it briefly with instance store, which is temporary.
  • He contrasts EBS with instance store, the temporary disk that disappears when the instance stops.

085 — EBS Introduction

  • An EBS volume is network-attached block storage. It persists if you stop the instance. It is not wiped by a reboot.
  • You can detach it and attach it to another instance in the same availability zone.
  • The root volume is the boot disk. Extra volumes are for data. He creates both in the lab.

EBS. Persistent block disks for EC2, in one availability zone, that survive a stop.

086 — EBS Volume Types

  • gp3 and gp2 are general-purpose SSD, the default for most disks including the labs. gp3 lets you set performance separately from size.
  • io1 and io2 are for databases that need a guaranteed number of IOPS. They cost more.
  • st1 is throughput HDD for big sequential files. sc1 is cold HDD, the cheapest, for data you rarely read.
  • He picks gp3 for the demo and says not to memorize prices, only which type is SSD, provisioned IOPS, or cheap HDD.

IOPS. How many read or write operations a disk can do per second.

087 — EBS Snapshots

  • A snapshot is a point-in-time copy of a volume, stored in S3 behind the scenes and billed as snapshot data.
  • He creates a snapshot from a volume, then creates a new volume from that snapshot, including in another availability zone.
  • Snapshots are incremental. Deleting a volume does not delete its snapshots. He deletes the demo snapshot on purpose.

EBS snapshot. A backup of an EBS volume that you can turn back into a volume.

088 — EBS Hands On

  • In EC2 he opens Volumes, creates a small gp3 volume in the instance's zone, and attaches it.

  • He detaches, deletes the extra volume, and deletes the snapshot. He does not delete the root volume of a running instance by mistake.

089 — EBS Summary

  • EBS is the durable disk. Instance store is the temporary disk that vanishes when the instance is stopped or terminated.
  • Pick gp3 unless you have a clear IOPS or throughput reason. Snapshot before risky changes.
  • Volumes are zonal. Snapshots can be copied and used to rebuild a volume.

Elastic File System (EFS)

090 — EFS Overview

  • EFS is a shared Linux file system. Many EC2 instances mount the same files at once. EBS does not do that.
  • He compares the two, then creates a file system and mounts it.
  • The lab mounts one file system from more than one Linux instance to prove it is shared.

091 — EFS Introduction

  • EFS speaks NFS and is built for Linux. It grows and shrinks as you add and delete files. You do not pick a disk size up front.
  • It lives in the VPC, across availability zones, so instances in different zones can mount it.
  • A Windows fleet that needs SMB is not this service. He will later point that at FSx for Windows.

EFS. A managed network file system that many Linux instances share.

092 — EFS vs EBS

  • EBS is one volume, one instance at a time, block device, zonal, and what you boot from.
  • EFS is many readers and writers, a directory you mount, regional, and not a boot disk.
  • EFS costs more per gigabyte and is the right tool when the files themselves must be shared. A database disk stays on EBS or on RDS.

093 — EFS Hands On

  • On Amazon Linux he installs the NFS client and mounts the file-system DNS name on a folder.
  • A file created on one instance shows up on another. He unmounts and deletes the file system afterward.

094 — EFS Summary

  • Shared Linux files go on EFS. A single server's disk stays on EBS.
  • Mount targets and security groups are the usual reason a mount fails.
  • You pay for the bytes stored, and the file system scales without a resize step.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Creating EFS with mount targets and mounting it from Linux with NFS.

Elastic Load Balancing (ELB)

095 — ELB Overview

  • A load balancer spreads requests across instances so one EC2 failure is not an outage.
  • He will separate the balancer types, health checks, and listeners, then create one.
  • He creates an Application Load Balancer in the lab and deletes it, because the balancer is billed by the hour.

096 — ELB Introduction

  • Clients hit one DNS name. The load balancer sends each request to a healthy instance.
  • He places the balancer in public subnets and the instances behind it. The instances no longer need to be reached directly.
  • A target group is the list of instances or IPs the balancer is allowed to send to.

Load balancer. A service that accepts traffic on one address and spreads it across healthy targets.

097 — Types of Load Balancers

  • An Application Load Balancer works at HTTP and HTTPS. It can route by path or host name. This is the one he wants for a website.
  • A Network Load Balancer works at TCP and UDP and holds a static IP. He uses it when the app is not HTTP, or when performance at layer 4 matters.
  • A Gateway Load Balancer is for inserting a firewall appliance in the traffic path. He mentions it so the acronym is not a surprise.
  • Classic Load Balancer is the old combined type. He says not to start anything new on it.

Application Load Balancer. The HTTP and HTTPS load balancer that can route by URL.

Network Load Balancer. The TCP and UDP load balancer, with a static IP.

098 — ELB Health Checks

  • The balancer asks each target for a page or a port on a timer. Failures in a row mark the target unhealthy and traffic stops going there.
  • He sets the path, the healthy threshold, and the interval. A wrong path marks every instance unhealthy even when the server is up.
  • Security groups must allow the balancer to reach that port.

099 — ELB Listeners and Target Groups

  • A listener is the port the balancer opens, such as 80 or 443, and the rule for where that traffic goes.
  • The target group holds the instances, the port on the instance, and the health check.
  • He registers two instances in one target group and points the listener at that group.

100 — ELB Hands On

  • He creates an Application Load Balancer in the default VPC, internet-facing, across two subnets.
  • He creates a target group, registers the demo instances, and sets the health check path.
  • The balancer DNS name serves the page. He deletes the balancer and target group at the end. Balancers are not free.

101 — ELB Best Practices

  • Use more than one availability zone. Turn cross-zone balancing on when you want even spread.
  • Keep health checks on, and only send traffic to targets that pass.
  • Terminate HTTPS on the balancer when you can, so the certificate lives in one place.
  • Sticky sessions pin a user to one instance. He says to use them only when the app requires it, because they unbalance the load.

102 — ELB Shared Responsibility Model

  • AWS runs the load-balancer service and replaces a failed balancer node.
  • You choose the type, the subnets, the certificates, the health checks, and which targets exist.
  • A target that is unhealthy because your app crashed is your problem to fix.

103 — ELB Summary

  • ALB for websites, NLB for TCP and UDP, Gateway Load Balancer for firewall appliances.
  • Listeners, target groups, and health checks are the three objects he configured.
  • Delete the balancer when the lab is over.

Auto Scaling Group (ASG)

104 — ASG Overview

  • An Auto Scaling group keeps the right number of EC2 instances running and changes that number with load.
  • He pairs it with the load balancer from the previous unit.
  • A launch template supplies the instance settings, and the group is meant to sit behind the load balancer.

105 — ASG Introduction

  • You give the group a launch template, a minimum, a desired count, and a maximum, and the subnets it may use.
  • If an instance fails its health check, the group replaces it. That is the self-healing part.
  • Scaling policies change the desired count. He will do manual, scheduled, dynamic, and predictive.

Auto Scaling group. A set of EC2 instances kept at a size you set, replaced when one fails, and resized by policy.

106 — Launch Templates

  • A launch template is the saved launch form: AMI, instance type, key pair, security group, and user data.
  • The Auto Scaling group uses the template whenever it starts an instance. You change the template, not each server by hand.
  • He versions the template. The group points at a version so a bad edit can be rolled back.

Launch template. The saved EC2 settings an Auto Scaling group uses to start instances.

107 — ASG Health Checks

  • EC2 health checks notice a failed hypervisor or instance status. ELB health checks notice an app that stopped answering.
  • He turns on ELB health checks so a broken web server is replaced, not just a crashed virtual machine.
  • The grace period stops the group from killing an instance that has not finished booting.

108 — Scaling Policies Introduction

  • Manual scaling is you changing the desired count.
  • Scheduled scaling is a clock, such as scale up at 9 a.m.
  • Dynamic scaling reacts to a metric such as CPU. Predictive scaling uses history to scale before the peak.

109 — Dynamic Scaling

  • Target tracking is the one he recommends: keep average CPU near a number, and the group adds or removes instances.
  • Step scaling jumps by bigger amounts when the metric is further past the threshold. Simple scaling is the older, coarser option.
  • Scale-in can remove servers. He sets a cooldown so the group does not flap.

110 — Scheduled Scaling

  • He writes a schedule with a cron-style time, a minimum, and a maximum. The office-hours website is the example.
  • Scheduled actions do not watch CPU. They run even if the metric is quiet.
  • Time is in UTC in the console. He tells you to convert before you save the action.

111 — Predictive Scaling

  • Predictive scaling reads the group's history and adds capacity ahead of a repeating pattern.
  • It needs history. A brand-new group has nothing to predict from, so he pairs it with target tracking.
  • He treats it as an extra, not a replacement, for dynamic scaling.

112 — ASG with Load Balancer

  • He attaches the Auto Scaling group to the target group. New instances are registered. Removed instances are deregistered.
  • The load balancer stops sending users to an instance before the group terminates it.
  • This pair, balancer plus group, is the pattern he calls a normal production web tier.

113 — ASG Hands On

  • He creates a launch template for Amazon Linux, t2.micro, with user data that starts a web server.
  • He creates a group with minimum 1, desired 1, maximum 2, in two subnets, attached to the target group.
  • He raises desired capacity, watches a second instance launch, then deletes the group so it terminates the instances.

114 — ASG Best Practices

  • Use a launch template, more than one subnet, and ELB health checks with a grace period.
  • Prefer target tracking. Set a maximum so a bug cannot launch a huge fleet.
  • Scale on a metric that matches the bottleneck. CPU is wrong if the app is stuck on the database.

115 — ASG Shared Responsibility Model

  • AWS starts and stops instances when a policy says so, and replaces a failed host.
  • You write the template, the min and max, the health check, and the metric. A bad user-data script is yours.
  • The bill for every instance the group starts is yours as well.

116 — Scaling Cooldown and Warm Up

  • Cooldown is the pause after a simple scaling action before another one is allowed.
  • Warm-up is the time a new instance is ignored by target tracking, because its CPU is not meaningful yet.
  • He sets them so a slow boot does not trigger a second scale-out.

117 — ASG Monitoring

  • The group shows current, desired, min, and max in the console. CloudWatch holds CPU and the group's own metrics.
  • He looks at "group desired capacity" and "group in service instances" when a scale-out did not happen.
  • An activity history tab lists every launch and termination and the error if a launch failed.

118 — ASG Quiz

  • Items contrast a launch template with a running instance, and ELB health checks with EC2 health checks.
  • Target tracking is the policy that aims at a metric. Scheduled scaling is the one with a clock.
  • The group, not the load balancer, is what creates and deletes the instances.

119 — ASG Termination Policies

  • When the group must shrink, a termination policy chooses which instance dies. The default balances availability zones first.
  • He mentions oldest launch template and closest to the next billing hour as other choices.
  • He leaves the default unless a lab has a reason not to.

120 — ASG Instance Refresh

  • Instance refresh replaces instances a few at a time with the new launch template, so you can roll out a new AMI without killing the site.
  • He sets a minimum healthy percentage so some capacity always stays in service.
  • This is his controlled replacement, as opposed to deleting the group.

121 — ASG Summary

  • A group plus a template plus a load balancer is how he keeps a web fleet alive and the right size.
  • Manual, scheduled, dynamic, and predictive are the four ways the size changes.
  • Always set a maximum, and delete the group when the lab ends so it does not keep launching.

122 — ASG Quiz

  • A second quiz mixes the policy types and asks who replaces a failed instance.
  • Warm-up and cooldown are not the same pause. He separates them.
  • The answer to "what starts the instance" is the Auto Scaling group using the launch template.

  • Building a launch template and a small Auto Scaling group behind a load balancer, then deleting the group.
  • Comparing target tracking, scheduled scaling, and predictive scaling.

Unit 16

S3 Advanced

132 — S3 Advanced Overview

  • He goes through storage classes, versioning, lifecycle, replication, encryption, and static website hosting.
  • Each feature is a console control on the bucket or the object.
  • Versioning, lifecycle rules, replication, encryption, and static website hosting each get a console pass.

133 — S3 Storage Classes

  • Standard is frequent access. Standard-IA and One Zone-IA cost less to store and more to read. One Zone-IA keeps data in a single zone.
  • Glacier Instant Retrieval, Glacier Flexible Retrieval, and Glacier Deep Archive are the archive end. Deeper means cheaper storage and slower or costlier retrieval.
  • Intelligent-Tiering watches access and moves the object between frequent and infrequent tiers for you.
  • He says durability of Standard is across zones. One Zone-IA is the class that gives that up.

S3 Intelligent-Tiering. A class that moves objects between access tiers based on use.

134 — S3 Versioning

  • Versioning keeps every write as a version. A delete adds a delete marker instead of erasing the bytes immediately.
  • He enables it on the bucket, uploads the same key twice, and shows both version IDs. He can restore by deleting the marker.
  • Suspending versioning stops new versions. It does not throw away old ones. Only a permanent delete of a version ID removes that data.

Versioning. Keeping prior writes of the same S3 key so you can restore them.

135 — S3 Lifecycle Policies

  • A lifecycle rule transitions objects to a cheaper class, or expires them, after a number of days.
  • He writes a rule: Standard to Standard-IA after 30 days, then to Glacier, then expire. Current and noncurrent versions can have different rules.
  • Transitions have minimum days. He checks the rule instead of moving objects by hand.

136 — S3 Replication

  • Replication copies objects to another bucket, in another region (CRR) or the same region (SRR). Versioning must be on for both buckets.
  • He needs an IAM role that S3 can use to write to the destination.
  • Existing objects are not copied unless he runs a batch replication. New uploads are what the rule covers.

Cross-region replication. Automatic copy of new S3 objects to a bucket in another region.

137 — S3 Encryption

  • SSE-S3 is encryption AWS manages with its own keys. It is the simple default he turns on.
  • SSE-KMS uses a key in KMS, so you can control who can use the key and see the use in CloudTrail. It can add a KMS cost.
  • SSE-C is a key you send with each request. Client-side encryption means you encrypt before the upload. He names both and does not build them.

SSE-S3. S3 encrypts the object with keys AWS handles.

SSE-KMS. S3 encrypts the object with a key you manage in KMS.

138 — S3 Static Website Hosting

  • He enables static website hosting and sets index.html and an error document.
  • The website endpoint is different from the REST object URL. Files must be readable by the public, which means a bucket policy and relaxing Block Public Access for that case only.
  • He says a real site should sit behind CloudFront. The bucket website is the simple version.

139 — S3 Access Points and Object Lambda

  • An access point is a named entry to a bucket with its own policy, so different apps do not share one bucket policy.
  • Object Lambda lets you run a Lambda function as the object is read, for example to redact a column. He describes it and does not require the lab.
  • Both are optional tools on top of a normal bucket.

140 — S3 Event Notifications

  • The bucket can send a message when an object is created or deleted. Targets he names are an SNS topic, an SQS queue, or a Lambda function.
  • He filters by prefix or suffix so only .jpg or only a folder triggers it.
  • This is the hook he uses later when a Lambda function runs on an upload.

141 — S3 Batch Operations

  • Batch Operations runs one action across a list of keys: copy, tag, restore from Glacier, or invoke Lambda.
  • He builds the list as a manifest. The job reports how many objects succeeded.
  • It is for millions of existing objects, where a lifecycle rule or a replication rule is the wrong tool.

142 — S3 Hands On Storage Classes

Scaling and High Availability

163 — Scaling and High Availability Overview

  • He ties together the load balancer and the Auto Scaling group and names vertical versus horizontal scaling.
  • High availability and fault tolerance are separated on purpose.
  • He reuses the load balancer and the Auto Scaling group as the concrete version of these ideas.

164 — Vertical vs Horizontal Scaling

  • Vertical scaling makes one machine bigger. It hits a maximum size and usually needs a restart.
  • Horizontal scaling adds machines. A load balancer spreads the work. This is what Auto Scaling does.
  • He says design the app so a user is not stuck to one machine, or horizontal scaling will break sessions.

Horizontal scaling. Adding more machines. A load balancer spreads traffic across them.

Vertical scaling. Making one machine larger.

165 — High Availability vs Fault Tolerance

  • High availability means a short interruption is acceptable as long as the service recovers, usually by running in two zones.
  • Fault tolerance means no noticeable interruption, which costs more because a full spare is already running.
  • A single availability zone is neither, once that zone fails.

High availability. The service recovers quickly from a failure, often by using a second zone.

Fault tolerance. The service continues without a visible interruption.

166 — Multi-AZ Architecture

  • He draws a load balancer in two subnets, instances in two zones, and a database with a standby in the second zone.
  • One zone can fail and the balancer sends traffic to the zone that remains.
  • Multi-AZ is inside one region. It is not a second region.

167 — Elasticity in Practice

  • Elasticity is the Auto Scaling group changing size. High availability is the second zone. He wants both words used correctly.
  • A fixed group of two instances is highly available and not elastic. A group that scales in one zone is elastic and not highly available.
  • Target tracking is the mechanism from unit 14 that he points back to.

168 — Scaling Databases Introduction

  • A server scales horizontally more easily than a database, because the database holds the shared state.
  • He previews read replicas for extra reads, and Multi-AZ for a standby. The full RDS unit comes later.
  • He warns against putting the only copy of the data on one EC2 disk.

169 — Disaster Recovery Concepts

  • A backup is a copy you can restore. A pilot light is a tiny always-on core you can scale up. A warm standby is a smaller full copy already running.
  • Multi-site is two full regions active. Cost rises as recovery gets faster.
  • He ties RPO to how much data you can lose and RTO to how long you can be down.

RPO. How much recent data you can afford to lose.

RTO. How long you can afford to be down.

170 — Caching for Scale

  • A cache answers repeated reads from memory so the database does not. He names ElastiCache as the service, taught in its own unit.
  • CloudFront caches content near users. That is a different cache, at the edge.
  • He says caching is how you scale reads before you scale the database.

171 — Decoupling for Scale

  • A queue between two parts means a traffic spike waits in the queue instead of crashing the slower part.
  • He names SQS as that queue and leaves the detail for the decoupling unit.
  • The idea he wants now: do not make every component call the next one in the same request.

172 — Scaling Quiz

  • Items contrast vertical and horizontal, high availability and fault tolerance, and backup versus warm standby.
  • Multi-AZ is the pattern for surviving one zone. A second region is disaster recovery, not the default Multi-AZ answer.
  • Elasticity is shrinking as well as growing.

173 — Designing a Scalable Web App

  • He walks one picture: Route 53 later, a load balancer now, an Auto Scaling group in two zones, S3 for files, a database with a standby.
  • Stateless web servers, health checks, and a maximum group size are the three design rules he repeats.
  • Nothing new is launched. This is the diagram that uses units 13, 14, and 15 together.

174 — Scaling Summary

  • Scale out with a load balancer and an Auto Scaling group. Use two zones for high availability.
  • Know RPO and RTO before you pick a disaster-recovery pattern.
  • Queues and caches are the next tools when the database or a slow task is the bottleneck.

  • Drawing vertical versus horizontal scaling, and high availability versus fault tolerance.
  • Placing a load balancer, an Auto Scaling group, and a Multi-AZ database on one diagram.

Relational database. Tables of rows and columns, related by keys, queried with SQL.

AWS Glue. Managed extract, transform, and load, plus a data catalog.

Caching Services

200 — Caching Services Overview

  • A cache holds answers in memory so a slower database is not hit for every read.
  • ElastiCache is the service. Memcached and Redis are the two engines.
  • The application must read and write the cache. Putting ElastiCache in the VPC does not make that happen by itself.

201 — Caching Quiz

  • The quiz plants ElastiCache as the in-memory answer, not S3, RDS, or Glue.
  • A cache can be stale. He wants that tradeoff named before the lecture.
  • DAX is the DynamoDB-specific cache he will mention beside ElastiCache.

202 — ElastiCache Introduction

  • ElastiCache is managed Memcached or Redis. You get a cluster in your VPC. The application must be written to read the cache.
  • AWS patches and can replace a node. You choose the engine, the node size, and what you cache.
  • It is not a durable system of record. The database still is.

ElastiCache. Managed in-memory cache, either Memcached or Redis.

203 — Memcached vs Redis

  • Memcached is a simple key-value cache. It scales out by adding nodes. It does not do replication or rich data types.
  • Redis does replication, persistence options, sorting, and pub/sub. He picks Redis when he needs a standby or anything beyond a plain cache.
  • Both live in memory. A full flush loses what was only in the cache.

204 — Caching Strategies

  • Lazy loading reads the database on a miss and then fills the cache. The first read is slow. Data can be stale until it expires.
  • Write-through writes the cache and the database together. Reads are warm. Writes are slower.
  • He sets a TTL so a cached value cannot live forever. TTL here is how long a cache entry is kept.

TTL. How long a cached value is allowed to live before it must be read again.

205 — Caching Summary

  • Put a cache in front of repeated reads. Do not use it as the only copy of the data.
  • Memcached for simple scale-out caching. Redis when you need replication or richer structures.
  • Lazy loading and write-through are the two patterns, and a TTL limits staleness.

  • Comparing Memcached and Redis and walking lazy loading versus write-through.
  • Placing ElastiCache beside RDS in the web-app diagram from the scaling unit.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Say what a cache is for, and what it must not be the only copy of.
  • Choose Memcached or Redis for a case he described.
  • Explain lazy loading, write-through, and a TTL.

217 — ECS

  • ECS schedules Docker containers on a cluster. It registers tasks, restarts them, and works with a load balancer.
  • The EC2 launch type means you manage the instances. The Fargate launch type means you do not. Fargate costs more and is less to operate.
  • He picks ECS when he wants AWS-native containers without running Kubernetes.

ECS. AWS container orchestration for Docker. Launch type is EC2 or Fargate.

218 — Hands On ECS

  • He creates a Fargate cluster, a task definition that runs the nginx image on port 80, and a service in the default VPC with a public IP.
  • The security group must allow HTTP. The task's public IP shows the nginx welcome page.
  • He force-deletes the service, deletes the cluster, and deregisters the task definition.

219 — Fargate

  • Fargate is the serverless compute for containers. It works with both ECS and EKS. You set CPU and memory for the task.
  • It places and scales the tasks. You do not patch an EC2 AMI for the cluster.
  • Each task is isolated. Billing is per second of CPU and memory the task used.

Fargate. Serverless compute that runs ECS or EKS tasks without EC2 instances you manage.

220 — Hands On ECS with EC2 and Fargate

  • He creates an EC2-backed cluster: an Auto Scaling group, Amazon Linux, a small instance, a key pair, and a security group. The root volume minimum is larger than a normal free-tier EC2 disk.
  • He then creates a second cluster with only Fargate, which has no instances to configure.
  • He deletes both clusters and shows the CloudFormation stack the EC2 cluster created.

221 — ECR

  • ECR is a private Docker registry. You push images there and ECS or EKS pulls them. He calls it the AWS-side Docker Hub.
  • IAM controls who can push and pull. Images can be scanned. Lifecycle policies can expire old images.
  • A task definition points at the image URI in ECR.

ECR. A managed registry for Docker images, used by ECS, EKS, and Fargate.

222 — Serverless

  • Serverless means you do not provision the servers. The servers still exist. AWS runs them.
  • Work is event-driven and scales to zero when nothing is happening. You pay for use, not for an idle instance.
  • Lambda is the example. Limits exist: a function has a maximum run time, so a days-long job is a bad fit.

Serverless. A model where AWS runs the servers and you pay for the work triggered, not for an idle machine.

223 — Lambda

  • You upload a function. A trigger runs it: an API call, an S3 upload, a queue message, or a schedule.
  • You are billed only while the code runs, by duration and memory. No trigger means no charge for compute.
  • The function needs an IAM role for whatever it touches. The default role cannot read your bucket.
  • He repeats the limit: short work. A long batch belongs on EC2, ECS, or a data service.

Lambda. A function AWS runs for you when an event arrives, then stops billing when it finishes.

224 — Hands On Lambda Function

  • He creates a function, picks a runtime such as Python, and lets the console make a basic execution role.
  • The built-in test event returns the sample message. He changes the code, deploys, and tests again.
  • He shows the monitor tab and CloudWatch Logs, then deletes the function.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Triggering a Lambda function from an S3 upload and reading the CloudWatch log.
  • Creating an ECS Fargate service that serves nginx, then deleting the cluster. Walking EKS and Lightsail without leaving them running.

Unit 22

DNS Route 53

225 — DNS Route 53 Overview

  • He previews DNS, then Route 53 as AWS's DNS service, record types, TTL, and routing policies.
  • The lab creates a hosted zone and walks every policy in the console without buying a domain.
  • He does not register a paid domain. The console work is a hosted zone and the routing-policy form.

226 — DNS

  • DNS turns a name such as www.example.com into an IP address. Browsers need the address, not the words.
  • He walks the lookup: a local resolver asks a root server, then a top-level-domain server (.com), then the authoritative server for the name.
  • The answer that comes back is the IP the browser connects to. Route 53 can be that authoritative server.

DNS. The system that turns a domain name into an IP address.

227 — Route 53

  • Route 53 is AWS's DNS. It can register a domain, host the records, and health-check endpoints.
  • A record holds a name, a type, a value, a TTL, and a routing policy. He says the policies are the exam-heavy part.
  • He describes Route 53 as highly available, with a service-level agreement of 100 percent for the DNS service.

Route 53. AWS DNS: domain registration, hosted records, health checks, and routing policies.

TTL. How long a DNS answer may be cached before resolvers must ask again.

228 — Record Types

  • An A record maps a name to an IPv4 address. An AAAA record maps a name to an IPv6 address.
  • A CNAME makes one name an alias of another name, used for subdomains. An NS record lists the name servers for the zone.
  • When you create a hosted zone, Route 53 gives you NS records to put at the registrar so the world asks Route 53.

A record. A DNS record that points a name at an IPv4 address.

CNAME. A DNS record that points a name at another name.

229 — TTL

  • A high TTL means resolvers cache the answer for a long time. Fewer queries hit Route 53. A change of IP is slow to spread.
  • A low TTL means changes spread quickly and Route 53 answers more queries, which costs more.
  • He uses a low TTL while cutting over a site, then raises it when the address is stable.

230 — Routing Policies

  • Simple routing returns the values on one record. If several values exist, the client picks. It does not health-check.
  • Weighted routing splits traffic by numbers you set. Latency routing sends the user to the region that answers fastest from where they are.
  • Failover routing sends traffic to the primary while its health check passes, and to the secondary when it fails.
  • Geolocation is based on the user's location, for language or for blocking a country. Geoproximity is based on where users and resources are, with a bias you set, and needs traffic flow.
  • IP-based routing uses a list of address ranges you provide. Multivalue returns several healthy records so the client can choose.

Latency routing. Route 53 sends the DNS answer toward the region with the lowest delay for that user.

Failover routing. Route 53 sends users to a secondary record when the primary health check fails.

231 — Hands On Route 53

  • He creates a public hosted zone for a sample name such as example.com. A private zone would be only for a VPC.
  • On a new record he walks simple, weighted, geolocation, latency, failover, IP-based, and geoproximity. Failover is where a health check stops being optional.
  • Alias records point at AWS resources such as a load balancer. He does not buy a domain or create a live record set.

232 — Best Practices and Shared Responsibility Model

  • Use alias records for AWS resources. Attach health checks to failover. Pick TTL on purpose. Lock the hosted zone down with IAM.
  • AWS runs the DNS network and answers queries. You own the records, the routing policy, the health checks, and the domain renewal.
  • DDoS protection of the DNS service is something AWS builds. Turning on extra protections such as Shield is a choice he returns to in security.

233 — Route 53 Quiz

  • The primary job in his answers is DNS and domain registration, not virtual machines and not streaming.
  • Lowest-latency region is latency routing. Geography and content rules are geolocation. Healthy endpoints depend on health checks and failover.
  • Round robin, random, and blue/green are not Route 53 routing policies. Blue/green is a deployment pattern.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Walking a DNS lookup, then every Route 53 routing policy in the console on a sample hosted zone.
  • Separating alias, TTL, and health checks.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Explain how a name becomes an IP address.
  • Choose simple, weighted, latency, failover, or geolocation for a case.
  • Say what a high TTL does to change speed and to query cost.
  • State what you configure in Route 53 and what AWS operates.

Unit 23

Global Services

234 — Global Services Overview

  • He previews CloudFront, Global Accelerator, Outposts, Local Zones, Wavelength, and a comparison of the first two.
  • These are the services that sit near users or even in your building, unlike a normal region deployment.
  • Outposts, which he names beside Local Zones, is AWS hardware that sits in your building.

235 — Global Services Quiz

  • A region is a geographic area with multiple availability zones. An edge location is what CloudFront uses to cache near users.
  • CloudFront is the content-delivery answer. S3 is global-ish storage but not a CDN. Snowball is not a delivery network.
  • He uses the quiz to separate "close to the user" from "a second full region."

236 — CloudFront

  • CloudFront caches content at edge locations so users download from a nearby site instead of the origin every time.
  • The origin can be an S3 bucket, an EC2 instance, a load balancer, or a server that is not on AWS.
  • It is for HTTP and HTTPS: websites, video, APIs, and files. He pairs it with Route 53 for the DNS name.

CloudFront. AWS's content delivery network. It caches HTTP content at edge locations.

237 — Global Accelerator

  • Global Accelerator puts two static anycast IP addresses in front of an application and carries traffic onto the AWS network as early as possible.
  • It does not cache pages. It routes TCP and UDP to the closest healthy endpoint, such as a Network Load Balancer or an EC2 instance.
  • He uses it for gaming, voice, and other non-cacheable traffic where a stable IP matters.

Global Accelerator. Static IP addresses that send TCP and UDP onto the AWS network toward the nearest healthy region.

238 — CloudFront vs Global Accelerator

  • CloudFront caches HTTP and HTTPS. Global Accelerator does not cache. It accelerates TCP and UDP, and can also carry HTTP when caching is not the point.
  • CloudFront is addressed by DNS. Global Accelerator gives you static IP addresses.
  • A media website points at CloudFront. A real-time game points at Global Accelerator.

239 — Hands On CloudFront

  • He opens an existing distribution and shows the domain name, the certificate, the price class, and that logging and cookies are off.
  • The origin is a small static site. He does not buy a domain or create a new distribution, to avoid the extra cost.
  • He shows the CloudFront hostname as the URL users would open, not the origin hostname.

240 — Hands On Global Accelerator

  • He starts an accelerator with IPv4, TCP port 80, a region endpoint, and a traffic dial of 100.
  • The console shows two static addresses and a DNS name. He then disables the accelerator and deletes it.
  • He says leaving an accelerator running is a real charge even with no users.

241 — Local Zones

  • A Local Zone is a small extension of a region in a specific city, for single-digit millisecond latency. Los Angeles attached to us-west-2 is his example.
  • You can run EC2, EBS, ECS, EKS, and RDS there without building your own edge site.
  • It is still AWS-operated. It is not a full region and not an edge cache.

Local Zone. A city-level extension of a region for very low latency compute and storage.

242 — S3 CORS

  • CORS is how a browser allows a page on one domain to read an object in a bucket on another domain.
  • He edits the bucket's permissions, opens the CORS section, and pastes a rule that lists the allowed origin and methods.
  • Without that rule the browser blocks the call even when the object is public. This is a browser rule, not an IAM grant.

CORS. A bucket rule that lets a web page on another domain read the bucket from a browser.

243 — AWS Wavelength

  • Wavelength places AWS compute inside a telecom provider's 5G network, so a phone app can reach it without crossing the public internet first.
  • You deploy EC2, EBS, and a VPC in a Wavelength Zone. The target latency he quotes is under about 10 milliseconds.
  • He aims it at real-time mobile and immersive apps, not at a normal website. That remains CloudFront.

Wavelength. AWS infrastructure inside a mobile carrier's network, for ultra-low-latency mobile apps.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Contrasting CloudFront's cache with Global Accelerator's static IPs, and deleting a demo accelerator.
  • Defining Local Zones and Wavelength as places even closer than a region.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Choose CloudFront or Global Accelerator for a workload.
  • Explain an edge location, a Local Zone, and a Wavelength Zone.
  • Say what an S3 CORS rule is for.

Storage Advanced

254 — Storage Advanced Overview

  • Beyond S3 and EBS he adds FSx file systems, Storage Gateway for hybrid disks, and the transfer tools: Transfer Family, DataSync, and App2Container.
  • The unit is which storage protocol you already speak.
  • EFS remains the simple shared Linux file system. FSx is what he uses when the protocol must be SMB, Lustre, or NetApp.

255 — FSx

  • FSx for Windows File Server speaks SMB for Windows apps. FSx for Lustre is the fast file system for high-performance computing and machine learning, and it connects to S3.
  • FSx for NetApp ONTAP adds snapshots, dedup, and NFS, SMB, and iSCSI. FSx for OpenZFS is the Linux and DevOps file system.
  • EFS remains the simple Linux NFS choice. FSx is the one that matches a specific existing file system.

FSx. A family of managed file systems: Windows, Lustre, NetApp ONTAP, and OpenZFS.

256 — Hands On FSx

  • He starts an FSx for Lustre wizard: a name, persistent SSD, and a throughput per terabyte. He points at scratch SSD as the temporary alternative.
  • He shows the same first step for Windows, ONTAP, and OpenZFS without launching the expensive file systems.
  • He cancels rather than create, and says these file systems bill as soon as they exist.

257 — AWS File Gateway

  • Storage Gateway's file gateway lets an on-premises server see S3 as an NFS or SMB share. A local cache holds the hot files.
  • The gateway runs as a virtual machine on-premises or on EC2. Volume gateway and tape gateway are the other modes, for block disks and for backup tapes.
  • It is a hybrid tool. If everything is already in AWS, you do not need it.

Storage Gateway. A hybrid appliance that presents S3, volumes, or virtual tapes to an on-premises network.

258 — Hands On Storage Gateway

  • He starts an S3 file gateway, sets the time zone, and chooses EC2 as the host platform with a VPC and a key pair.
  • The console notes that FSx File Gateway is closed to new customers. He stays with the S3 file gateway screens.
  • He does not finish a gateway that would keep an EC2 host running.

259 — Transfer Family

  • Transfer Family is managed SFTP, FTPS, and FTP in front of S3 or EFS. Clients keep their existing file-transfer tools.
  • Users can authenticate with IAM, Active Directory, or a custom provider. He treats SFTP as the encrypted choice of the three.
  • It is not a migration robot. It is a server the client connects to.

Transfer Family. Managed FTP, FTPS, and SFTP endpoints that store files in S3 or EFS.

260 — DataSync

  • DataSync copies data quickly between on-premises storage and S3, EFS, or FSx, or between AWS services and regions.
  • An agent reads the source. The service verifies the copy and encrypts it in transit. You do not write the copy scripts.
  • He calls it faster and more managed than Transfer Family, and more expensive, and he uses it for migrations and ongoing sync.

261 — App2Container

  • App2Container is a command-line tool that analyzes a Java or .NET app on a virtual machine and produces a container image.
  • The output can run on ECS, on EKS, or as a plain Docker image. It is a modernization step, not a registry and not a load balancer.
  • He flags the phrase "command line" plus "containerize an existing app" as the exam hint for this tool.

App2Container. A command-line tool that turns an existing Java or .NET application into a container.

262 — Best Practices

  • Again his balancer checklist, applied to storage-backed apps: health checks, multi-zone targets, TLS at the load balancer, and Auto Scaling behind it.
  • Sticky sessions only when a user must stay on one instance. Route 53 when failover is by DNS.
  • No new storage setting is introduced in this recording.

263 — Storage Advanced Quiz

  • App2Container is the answer for discovering and containerizing an app that already runs on a virtual machine.
  • SFTP is the encrypted Transfer Family protocol among the options he gives. FTP is the plain one.
  • Storage Gateway is the hybrid answer when an on-premises share must land in S3.

  • CloudWatch is metrics and alarms. CloudTrail is the audit log of API calls. EventBridge routes events. X-Ray traces a request. Health Dashboard shows AWS service health.

Unit 27

Decoupling

275 — Decoupling Overview

  • He previews SQS, SNS, Kinesis and its four forms, and Amazon MQ.
  • The goal is parts of the system that can fail or slow down without taking the others with them.
  • He will treat a queue, a notification topic, and a data stream as different tools, not as three names for one service.

276 — Decoupling Quiz

  • SNS is the pub/sub answer. Kinesis is the real-time stream. Amazon MQ is the broker when the protocol is AMQP, MQTT, or a similar existing standard.
  • SQS is a queue, not a fan-out. It does support FIFO queues. It does not push one message to many subscribers. That is SNS.
  • He uses the quiz to keep queue, topic, and stream as three different words.

277 — Decoupling

  • Coupled components wait on each other. If one stops, the next stops. Decoupling puts a buffer or a message between them.
  • He says you can then scale or patch one piece without redeploying the whole system.
  • The buffer is the queue or the stream from the following lectures. The idea is the lecture.

Decoupling. Separating components so one can fail or scale without the others depending on its internals.

278 — SQS

  • SQS is a managed queue. Producers send messages. Consumers pull them when they are ready. The queue holds the backlog.
  • A standard queue scales and may deliver more than once. A FIFO queue preserves order and avoids duplicates, with a lower throughput.
  • He places it between a web tier and a slow task so a spike becomes a longer queue, not crashed servers.
  • Visibility timeout hides a message while a consumer works on it, so another consumer does not take it too.

SQS. A managed message queue. Senders add messages. Workers pull them.

279 — SNS

  • SNS is pub/sub. A publisher sends to a topic. Every subscriber gets a copy: email, SMS, an SQS queue, or a Lambda function.
  • He uses a topic for "something happened" notifications. He uses a queue when exactly one worker should do the job.
  • A common pattern he names is SNS fan-out into several SQS queues.

SNS. A managed publish-and-subscribe service. One message can reach many subscribers.

280 — Hands On SNS

  • He creates a standard topic, then on an S3 bucket's properties he adds an event notification for object-created events aimed at that topic.
  • He pastes the topic ARN. The bucket needs permission to publish to the topic.
  • He keeps the demo small and deletes the topic when the screen has made the point.

281 — AWS Kinesis

  • Kinesis Data Streams takes in a high volume of events, holds them in shards, and lets consumers such as Lambda read them.
  • Producers are applications, logs, clicks, or IoT devices. Consumers process the stream. S3 or Redshift can store the result.
  • It is for continuous data, not for one job message. That remains SQS.

Kinesis Data Streams. A real-time stream that buffers events for multiple consumers.

282 — Kinesis Types

  • Data Firehose loads a stream into S3, Redshift, or OpenSearch, and can transform it with Lambda. You do not manage shards.
  • Data Analytics runs SQL or Flink on a stream for things like a running total or fraud detection.
  • Video Streams ingests video from cameras. He keeps Data Streams as the build-it-yourself option and Firehose as the delivery option.

Kinesis Data Firehose. A stream that captures data and delivers it to storage or a warehouse with little setup.

283 — Amazon MQ

  • Amazon MQ is a managed message broker for applications that already speak ActiveMQ or RabbitMQ protocols such as AMQP, MQTT, and OpenWire.
  • He uses it to lift an existing broker into AWS. A new design that can choose freely uses SQS or SNS instead.
  • It still decouples producers and consumers. The reason to pick it is compatibility, not a new messaging idea.

Amazon MQ. A managed ActiveMQ or RabbitMQ broker for applications that need those protocols.

Unit 28

Networking in AWS

285 — Networking in AWS Overview

  • He builds a VPC from empty: subnets, an internet gateway, a NAT gateway, flow logs, peering, endpoints, and a transit gateway.
  • Almost every EC2 and RDS lab already asked for a VPC. This unit is what that choice meant.
  • Security groups and network ACLs are both firewalls. He uses security groups on the instance for almost every lab.

286 — VPC

  • A VPC is your private network in one region. You pick an address range and launch EC2, RDS, and Lambda into it.
  • Subnets split that range. Route tables decide where packets go. An internet gateway is the door to the public internet.
  • A security group is a stateful firewall on the instance. A network ACL is a stateless firewall on the subnet. He will use security groups most of the time.

VPC. Your isolated virtual network in one AWS region.

Security group. A stateful virtual firewall attached to an instance or similar resource.

287 — Hands On VPC Peering

  • He creates a second VPC with a different CIDR range, then a peering connection: requester VPC, same account, same region, acceptor VPC.
  • He shows the connection as pending until accepted, and the two VPC IDs on the peering page.
  • Routes still have to be added in each route table before instances can actually talk. Creating the peering object is only the first step.

288 — Transit Gateway

  • Peering is one pair of VPCs, and it does not transitive-route through a third. Many peerings become a mesh.
  • A transit gateway is a hub. Each VPC, and an on-premises network, attaches to it and routes through the hub.
  • He uses peering for two VPCs and a transit gateway once there are many.

Transit gateway. A hub that connects many VPCs and on-premises networks without a mesh of peerings.

289 — VPC Endpoints

  • A gateway or interface endpoint lets a VPC reach services such as S3 without going out to the public internet.
  • A gateway endpoint is for S3 and DynamoDB and is a route in the route table. An interface endpoint is a private address in a subnet for most other services.
  • He uses endpoints so private subnets can reach AWS APIs without a NAT gateway, when an endpoint exists for that service.

VPC endpoint. A private path from a VPC to an AWS service that does not cross the public internet.

290 — Shared Responsibility Model

  • AWS runs the network fabric, the internet gateway service, and the hypervisor. You design the CIDR, the subnets, the routes, and the security groups.
  • A route that accidentally opens a database subnet to the world is your configuration.
  • The same split he used for EC2 applies: AWS operates the feature, you decide how it is wired.

291 — Hands On VPC

  • He creates a VPC with a manual IPv4 CIDR, leaves IPv6 off, and keeps the other options default.
  • The VPC page shows the ID, the CIDR, and an empty flow-log list.
  • He will add subnets and gateways in the following labs rather than using the "create everything" wizard only.

292 — VPC and Subnets

  • A public subnet has a route to an internet gateway. A private subnet does not. Databases go in the private subnet.
  • Each subnet sits in one availability zone. Two zones means at least two subnets.
  • A NAT gateway in a public subnet lets private instances start outbound connections, such as updates, without accepting inbound ones from the internet.

Public subnet. A subnet whose route table sends internet traffic to an internet gateway.

NAT gateway. A managed gateway that lets private instances make outbound internet connections.

293 — Hands On Subnets

  • In the new VPC he creates a subnet, leaves the zone as no preference or picks one, and sets a CIDR inside the VPC range.
  • The subnet page shows the VPC, the CIDR, and the zone it landed in.
  • He says the route table, not the subnet object, is what will later make it public.

294 — VPC Flow Logs

  • Flow logs capture accepted and rejected IP traffic on a VPC, a subnet, or a network interface. They are for troubleshooting and security review, not for blocking.
  • He can send them to S3 or to CloudWatch Logs. They do not include the payload, only the flow: ports, addresses, and whether it was allowed.
  • A security group still does the blocking. The log only records.

VPC flow log. A record of IP traffic to and from network interfaces in a VPC.

295 — Hands On Flow Logs

  • He creates a flow log for all traffic, with a ten-minute aggregation window, and a destination of CloudWatch Logs or an S3 bucket ARN.
  • The console can create the IAM role that writes the logs. He leaves extra metadata off.
  • He deletes the flow log after the demo so log storage does not continue.

296 — Internet Gateway

  • An internet gateway attaches to one VPC and allows public subnets to talk to the internet. It is highly available and does not do network address translation.
  • Only one internet gateway is attached to a VPC. Instances still need a public IP and a route of 0.0.0.0/0 to that gateway.
  • A NAT gateway is the separate object for private subnets. He tells you not to mix the two names on the exam.

297 — Hands On Internet Gateway

  • He creates an internet gateway and attaches it to the demo VPC. The attach list only offers VPCs that do not already have one.
  • He then starts a NAT gateway in the subnet he created. A NAT gateway needs that subnet and an Elastic IP, and it is billed.
  • He deletes the NAT gateway and detaches the internet gateway when the screens are done.

298 — VPC Peering

  • Peering connects two VPCs over private addresses. Traffic does not cross the public internet, a VPN, or a NAT gateway.
  • CIDR ranges must not overlap. Peering is not transitive: A peered to B, and B peered to C, does not let A talk to C.
  • It can cross regions and, with the right setup, accounts. Route tables on both sides must point at the peering connection.

VPC peering. A private network link between two VPCs.

299 — Networking Quiz

  • An internet gateway connects a VPC to the internet. Route 53, not the gateway, manages DNS names.
  • A NAT gateway lets a private instance reach out without accepting inbound connections. A security group is stateful and applies to the instance, not the subnet.
  • A network ACL is the stateless subnet firewall. Flow logs record traffic. They are not the firewall.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Creating a VPC, a subnet, an internet gateway, a peering connection, and a flow log, then removing the billed pieces.
  • Drawing public versus private subnets and peering versus a transit gateway.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Build a VPC with a public and a private subnet and say which route makes a subnet public.
  • Explain an internet gateway, a NAT gateway, a security group, and a network ACL.
  • Choose peering or a transit gateway, and say what a VPC endpoint avoids.

AI and ML Services

300 — AI and ML Services Overview

  • He treats this unit as a recognition list. Most services are APIs you call, not models you train.

  • Except for SageMaker, these are APIs you call. You do not train a model to use them.

301 — AWS Recognition

  • Rekognition finds faces, objects, text, and scenes in images and video. It can compare a face to a collection you stored.
  • Use cases he names are identity checks, moderation of uploads, and retail analytics.
  • You do not train a model first. You send the image to the API.

Rekognition. An image and video analysis API for faces, objects, text, and scenes.

302 — Forecast

  • Forecast predicts a number over time, such as demand, from history. It picks a model for you.
  • He calls it SaaS: you give it data and get a forecast. SageMaker is where you would build that model yourself.
  • Retail demand and staffing are the examples.

Forecast. A managed time-series forecasting service.

303 — AWS Kendra

  • Kendra searches your documents, wikis, and buckets and answers a natural-language question from that material.
  • It connects to sources such as S3 and SharePoint. It is not a general web search engine.
  • He uses it for an internal policy or knowledge-base search.

Kendra. An enterprise search service that answers questions from your own documents.

304 — AWS Textract

  • Textract reads text, forms, and tables out of scans and PDFs, including handwriting. It is OCR plus structure.

Textract. A service that extracts text, forms, and tables from documents and images.

306 — Transcribe

  • Transcribe turns speech into text, from a recording or a live stream, and can label different speakers.
  • Subtitles, call-center transcripts, and meeting notes are the uses. It can also flag sentiment at a basic level in that transcript.

Transcribe. Speech-to-text, including live audio and multiple speakers.

307 — Hands On Transcribe

308 — AWS Polly

  • Polly turns text into spoken audio, with many voices and languages, and can save an MP3.
  • Audiobooks, video voiceover, and screen readers are the uses.
  • You send text. You get audio. There is no training step.

Polly. Text-to-speech that returns spoken audio.

309 — Hands On AWS Polly

310 — AWS Translate

  • Translate is neural machine translation for text, in real time or as a batch of documents stored in S3.
  • He names multilingual sites and support chats. Custom terminology keeps brand names consistent.
  • It translates text. It does not transcribe audio. That is Transcribe first, then Translate.

Translate. A managed text translation service.

311 — Hands On Translate

312 — AWS Lex and Connect

Lex. A service for building chat and voice bots with intents and slots.

Connect. A cloud contact center that can use Lex bots.

313 — AWS Comprehend

  • Comprehend reads text and returns sentiment, entities such as people and places, key phrases, and the language.
  • It does not turn audio into text. You send it text, often from Transcribe.

Comprehend. Natural-language processing on text: sentiment, entities, and key phrases.

314 — AWS SageMaker

  • SageMaker is where you prepare data, train a model, and deploy it. Studio is the web IDE. Data Wrangler cleans data.

SageMaker. The platform for building, training, and deploying your own machine-learning models.

Unit 30

Security Services

315 — Security Services Overview

  • He frames the unit as "which security service does this job," because the exam asks that more than it asks you to configure them.
  • DDoS and the network firewall come first, then detection, audit, keys, and certificates.
  • The skill he wants is matching: read the job in the question and name the one service that does it.

316 — DDoS

  • A distributed denial-of-service attack floods a target with traffic from many sources so real users cannot get through.
  • He describes botnets and a sudden traffic spike that is not customers. The service that absorbs this at the edge is Shield, taught in the lab.
  • A firewall rule is not the whole answer, because the flood can arrive before your instance sees it.

DDoS. An attack that overwhelms a service with traffic from many sources so legitimate users cannot use it.

317 — Artifact

  • Artifact is the portal of AWS compliance reports: SOC, ISO, and similar documents, plus agreements you can accept.
  • It does not scan your instances and it does not store your application logs. It stores AWS's own compliance paperwork.
  • The exam phrase he wants is "compliance reports and agreements."

Artifact. The library of AWS compliance reports and agreements.

318 — GuardDuty

  • GuardDuty watches CloudTrail, VPC flow logs, and DNS logs for suspicious activity such as odd API calls or a compromised instance.
  • It uses threat intelligence and machine learning to raise findings. It does not patch the instance.
  • He treats a finding as the start of an investigation, which is Detective's job if you need the root cause.

GuardDuty. A threat-detection service that analyzes account and network logs for suspicious activity.

319 — Amazon Inspector

  • Inspector scans EC2, container images in ECR, and Lambda for known software vulnerabilities and for network exposure.
  • It reports a CVE match. It does not fix the package. You patch or you rebuild the image.
  • He separates it from GuardDuty: Inspector looks at software versions and exposure, not at attacker behavior in the logs.

Inspector. A scanner for software vulnerabilities and unintended network exposure.

320 — AWS Config

  • Config records how resources are configured over time and can check those configurations against rules.
  • He uses it to answer "when did this security group change" and "is every bucket encrypted." CloudTrail says who called the API. Config says what the resource looked like.
  • Rules can flag a drift. They do not by themselves undo it unless you add automation.

AWS Config. A record of resource configuration over time, with rules that check compliance.

321 — AWS Macie

  • Macie uses machine learning to find sensitive data, such as personal information and keys, in S3 buckets.
  • It alerts you to a bucket that is open or that contains data you did not mean to store there.
  • It is an S3 data-privacy tool, not a general malware scanner.

Macie. A service that finds sensitive data in S3.

322 — AWS Detective

  • Detective investigates a finding. It pulls related events so you can see how an alert, often from GuardDuty, came about.
  • It does not replace GuardDuty. GuardDuty detects. Detective helps you trace.
  • He also points it at security findings from the other tools in this unit.

Detective. An investigation tool for the root cause of a security finding.

323 — AWS Abuse

  • AWS Abuse is where you report misuse of AWS: phishing sites, malware, cryptocurrency mining, port scans, or someone launching attacks from AWS addresses.
  • It is a reporting process, not a firewall you attach to a subnet.
  • He lists phishing and DDoS launched from AWS as the typical reports.

324 — Root User Privileges

  • Only the root user can close the account, change billing, and perform a short list of account-level actions such as some support and Artifact steps.
  • He repeats the best practice from unit 06: MFA on root, no root access keys, and an IAM admin for daily work.
  • An IAM user with AdministratorAccess still cannot do every root-only action. He tells you not to be surprised by that.

325 — Shared Responsibility Model

  • The recording repeats the infrastructure split. AWS secures the data centers, the hardware, and the availability of security services such as Shield and KMS.
  • You turn the services on, write the rules, classify the data, and patch what you run on EC2.
  • Turning on GuardDuty is your decision. Operating the detection platform is AWS.

326 — Hands On AWS Shield

  • He launches a small EC2 instance, then opens the Shield network-security view and starts a network analysis.
  • The result lists security groups and resources with a severity related to DDoS exposure, including the new instance.
  • Shield Standard is the automatic edge protection. He describes Shield Advanced as the paid version with more help and reporting. He terminates the demo instance.

AWS Shield. Protection against DDoS. Standard is on by default. Advanced is the paid offering.

327 — Hands On WAF

  • He creates a web ACL, chooses a content site, attaches an existing CloudFront distribution, and looks at the managed rule packs and their cost.
  • WAF rules block or count HTTP requests by IP, pattern, or a managed rule such as common exploits.
  • He does not leave a paid rule pack attached. WAF is for HTTP. Shield is for the flood underneath.

WAF. A web application firewall that filters HTTP requests by rules you attach to CloudFront, a load balancer, or API Gateway.

328 — Network Firewall

  • Network Firewall is a managed firewall inside a VPC. Stateful rules track connections. Stateless rules filter packets.
  • It can filter domains and run intrusion detection and prevention. It is not the same as a security group, which is only on the resource.
  • He places it on the path between subnets or out to the internet when security groups are not enough.

Network Firewall. A managed VPC firewall with stateful and stateless rules and intrusion detection.

329 — KMS and CloudHSM

  • KMS creates and stores encryption keys and lets S3, EBS, RDS, and Lambda use them. Keys can rotate. Use is logged in CloudTrail.
  • CloudHSM is a dedicated hardware module in your VPC for when you must manage the hardware-backed keys yourself.
  • He picks KMS unless a rule forces a hardware module you operate.

KMS. Managed encryption keys for AWS services and your applications.

CloudHSM. A dedicated hardware security module you run inside a VPC.

330 — Hands On KMS

  • He creates a symmetric key for encrypt and decrypt, names an alias, and attaches an IAM user and a role that may use it.
  • He reviews the key policy and finishes. The key then appears in the list.
  • He says to schedule deletion if the key was only a demo, and that deletion has a waiting period.

331 — ACM

  • Certificate Manager issues and renews public TLS certificates for names you control, and can attach them to a load balancer, CloudFront, or API Gateway.
  • Private certificates are for internal names. You stop buying and renewing certificates by hand.
  • It does not scan for vulnerabilities. It proves a name and encrypts the connection.

ACM. Managed TLS certificates and their renewal, for AWS front doors such as load balancers and CloudFront.

332 — Secrets Manager

  • Secrets Manager stores database passwords, API keys, and tokens. It can rotate a database password on a schedule.
  • A secret has versions, so an application can move from the old password to the new one.
  • He prefers it to a password written in a config file or baked into an AMI.

Secrets Manager. A store for passwords and keys that can rotate them.

333 — Hands On Secrets Manager

  • He starts a secret for an RDS database, types a username and password, and selects a KMS key.
  • The wizard wants an existing database. He shows the RDS create screen for a free-tier MySQL instance and why the secret cannot be finished without it.
  • He tells you to delete both the database and the secret if you completed the wizard.

334 — Security Services Quiz

  • Secrets in the question means Secrets Manager. API activity means CloudTrail. Encrypting data with keys means KMS.
  • Blocking bad HTTP requests with rules means WAF. Scanning for software vulnerabilities means Inspector.
  • GuardDuty is the suspicious-activity answer. Config is the configuration-history answer. Macie is the sensitive-data-in-S3 answer.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Running a Shield network analysis and starting a WAF web ACL on CloudFront.
  • Creating a KMS key and walking a Secrets Manager secret, Artifact, and the detection tools as a matching list.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Match Shield, WAF, GuardDuty, Inspector, Config, Macie, Detective, KMS, ACM, and Secrets Manager to a job.
  • Explain DDoS in one sentence and which service absorbs it.
  • Say what only the root user can do, and what CloudTrail records that Config does not.

Unit 31

Management and Billing Console

335 — Management and Billing Console Overview

  • He previews Organizations, service control policies, consolidated billing, Control Tower, Resource Access Manager, and the cost tools.
  • The unit is how a company runs many accounts and watches the bill.
  • Cost Explorer, Budgets, and Cost Anomaly Detection are three different ways to look at the same bill.

336 — Billing and Management Console Quiz

  • Cost Explorer is the interactive cost graph. Consolidated billing is one payment for many accounts. Control Tower is the landing zone with guardrails.
  • Service control policies limit what an account can do. Organizations manages the accounts. Trusted Advisor is the best-practice checker.
  • None of these replace IAM. A policy in Organizations can only narrow permissions.

337 — AWS Organizations

  • Organizations groups accounts under a management account. Organizational units are folders of accounts.
  • Service control policies attach to an organization, an OU, or an account. Consolidated billing rolls the charges to one payer.
  • He uses separate accounts for security boundaries, then Organizations so they are not a pile of unrelated logins.

AWS Organizations. A way to group and centrally manage multiple AWS accounts.

338 — AWS Trusted Advisor

  • Trusted Advisor checks the account against best practices in cost, performance, security, fault tolerance, and service limits.
  • Examples he gives: idle EC2, an unattached disk, a missing backup, a public access setting, a service limit almost reached.
  • It recommends. It does not change the resource until you do. More checks unlock with higher support plans.

Trusted Advisor. Checks that recommend cost, security, performance, and fault-tolerance fixes.

339 — SCPs

  • A service control policy sets the maximum permissions in an account. It does not grant access. IAM still has to grant it.
  • If the SCP denies an action, even the account's root user cannot do it. If IAM allows and the SCP allows, the action proceeds.
  • He uses SCPs for guardrails such as "no one in this OU may leave a region" or "no one may turn off CloudTrail."

Service control policy. A policy in Organizations that limits the maximum permissions of an account. It does not grant permissions.

340 — Consolidated Billing

  • One management account pays for every member account. You still see charges per account.
  • Combined usage can reach a volume discount that no single account would reach alone. There is no fee for the billing feature itself.
  • Paying together does not merge IAM. Accounts stay isolated except where you share on purpose.

Consolidated billing. One bill for all accounts in an organization, with per-account detail.

341 — AWS Control Tower

  • Control Tower sets up a multi-account landing zone with Organizations, CloudTrail, and Config already arranged.
  • Guardrails are the ongoing rules. Account Factory is how you vend a new account that matches the blueprint. A dashboard shows compliance.
  • He picks Organizations alone for a simple group of accounts, and Control Tower when the question says best-practice landing zone or guardrails.

Control Tower. A service that sets up and governs a multi-account environment with guardrails.

342 — AWS Resource Access Manager

  • Resource Access Manager shares a resource, such as a subnet, a transit gateway, or a license configuration, with another account or OU.
  • You pay for the resource, not for the share. The other account uses it without you copying it.
  • It is not a way to share an IAM user. Identities stay in their own account.

Resource Access Manager. Shares selected AWS resources with other accounts in your organization.

343 — AWS Cost Explorer

  • Cost Explorer graphs cost by time, service, account, or tag, and can forecast from recent use.
  • He uses it to find which service dominates the bill and to group by a project tag.
  • It is historical and forecast analysis. It does not stop a resource that is over budget. Budgets does the alerting.

Cost Explorer. Graphs and forecasts of AWS cost and usage.

344 — AWS Budgets

  • A budget tracks cost or usage against a number you set, for an account, a service, or a tag. SNS or email fires at 80 percent or at 100 percent.
  • He also mentions budgets for Savings Plans and Reserved Instance coverage.
  • A budget warns you. It does not by itself terminate the expensive resource.

AWS Budgets. Alerts when cost or usage is forecast to cross a limit you set.

345 — AWS Cost Anomaly Detection

  • Anomaly Detection watches spending and alerts when a service or account jumps away from its normal pattern, using machine learning.
  • A sudden Auto Scaling event is the kind of spike he wants flagged the day it happens, not at the end of the month.
  • You can scope it by service, account, or cost category, and send the alert through SNS.

Cost Anomaly Detection. Alerts on unusual AWS spending compared with the account's normal pattern.

346 — Hands On of Full AWS Billing and Cost Management Console

  • He opens the billing home page: month-to-date cost, forecast, and a budget that is already over its one-dollar limit.
  • In Cost Explorer he groups by service and shows EC2 as the largest bar, then points at the budgets and anomaly pages.
  • He treats the console as the place you check before you leave a lab running.

What he is doing in this unit

  • Walking Organizations, SCPs, and the billing console, including Cost Explorer grouped by service.
  • Matching Trusted Advisor, Control Tower, Budgets, and Anomaly Detection to a sentence each.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Explain an organization, an OU, consolidated billing, and what an SCP does not grant.
  • Choose Cost Explorer, Budgets, or Cost Anomaly Detection for a cost question.
  • Say what Trusted Advisor and Control Tower add on top of Organizations.
