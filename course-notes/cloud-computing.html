Cloud Computing — lecture notes

These notes follow the Tutedude cloud computing lectures in folder order. Each lecture is a short reading of what that recording explains and which console clicks it shows. Names are written in their usual AWS form (EC2, S3, IAM, VPC, EBS, RDS, ELB, Route 53, CloudFront, Lambda, SNS, SQS, and the service models IaaS, PaaS, SaaS). A few caption files repeat an earlier talk or carry the wrong title; the note says what the audio actually covers.

The course splits into cloud ideas, then AWS from the account and IAM through compute, storage, databases, containers, DNS, global edge, infrastructure as code, monitoring, decoupling, VPC networking, AI services, security, and billing. He keeps telling you to delete what you create. Free-tier templates and “do not click create” moments are called out where he stops on purpose.

04 — Origin of cloud computing

Where shared computing came from, and why virtual machines made renting a server possible.

004 Origin of cloud

  • He starts with John McCarthy’s time-sharing on mainframes in the 1960s: many people using one large machine.
  • By the 1990s the public internet made remote servers reachable; he marks 1999 as the stretch when “cloud” ideas showed up in products, before AWS.
  • AWS is the later, large public example, not the invention of shared computing.
  • The thread is: one computer, many users, then many computers you reach over a network instead of buying.

005 Virtualization

  • Virtualization is the pillar he names: without it there is no cloud, because one physical server has to look like many machines.
  • He uses the mirror image as the picture of “virtual”: you can see it and use it, and it is not a second physical computer.
  • A hypervisor sits on the hardware and hands each guest its own CPU, memory, and disk view.
  • That is how a provider rents slices of a data-center server instead of shipping you a box.

006 Benefits of virtualization

  • Cost: you rent hardware in someone else’s data center instead of buying servers, disks, and network gear.
  • Flexibility: the same provider can offer storage, databases, networking, security, monitoring, and compute from that shared pool.
  • Isolation: one virtual machine can be restarted or resized without rebuilding the physical host.
  • Speed: a new machine is a configuration, not a purchase order and a rack.

007 Uses and future aspects of cloud computing

  • Uses he lists: data storage and backups (Google Drive, S3), web hosting on AWS, Azure, or Heroku, and software you only use, such as Gmail, Zoom, and Salesforce.
  • Disaster recovery: a copy of the environment lives in another place, so a failed office does not erase the system.
  • He also points at future use in more software, more remote work, and more services that start as a click instead of a purchase.
  • The pattern is the same: files, sites, and finished applications move off the machine in front of you.

What he is doing in this unit

Talking through history and definitions. No console.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Place time-sharing, the internet, and AWS on one timeline.
  • Explain virtualization as many logical machines on one physical server.
  • Name storage, hosting, finished software, and disaster recovery as early uses.

05 — Traditional IT and cloud

008 Traditional IT approach overview

  • This unit is the contrast: what on-premises IT is, what breaks, what cloud adds, then a side-by-side table.
  • He will define owning the servers, then the bills, the waiting, and the unused capacity.
  • Key features and benefits come next, and he warns they will sound similar but are not the same list.
  • The table at the end is the revision sheet for ownership, cost, and scaling.

009 Problems with the traditional IT approach

  • Traditional IT, which he also calls on-premises, means the company buys and runs servers, network gear, storage, and software in its own rooms.
  • You pay capital cost up front, then power, cooling, space, and people, whether the servers are busy or idle.
  • Growing means buying more hardware and waiting for it; shrinking leaves you with boxes you still own.
  • A failure in that one room is your outage, because the spare copy is also something you had to build.

010 Cloud computing key features

  • On-demand self-service: you take one server and a small database for a small site, and you do it yourself, without a vendor ticket.
  • He ties each feature back to a traditional problem: no unused hardware sitting paid for, and no weeks of procurement.
  • Broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, and measured service are the rest of the set he walks: reach it over the network, share the pool, grow and shrink, and pay for what the meter shows.
  • Elasticity is the feature he uses to answer “what if traffic spikes tonight.”

011 Benefits of cloud

  • The phrase he wants remembered: trade capital expense (CapEx) for operational expense (OpEx). You stop buying the routers and disks and start paying a bill.
  • Benefits sound like the features, but here he is talking about the result: less money locked in hardware, faster start, and someone else patching the building.
  • You can stop a resource and stop most of its cost, which you cannot do with a server you already bought.
  • Global reach is a benefit he separates from the feature list: the same account can place work near users.

012 Tabular comparison

  • Infrastructure ownership: the organization in the traditional column, the provider (he names AWS, Azure, GCP as the big three) in the cloud column.
  • Upfront cost is the next row: buy the estate, or pay as you go.
  • He continues down the table for maintenance, scaling, and who shows up when a disk fails.
  • Use this lecture as the one-page contrast before the service-model unit.

What he is doing in this unit

Comparing two columns on a slide. No console.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Define on-premises as equipment the company owns and runs.
  • List on-demand, elasticity, pooling, and measured use as the features.
  • Say CapEx versus OpEx in his words.
  • Fill the ownership and upfront-cost rows of his table.

06 — Cloud service models

013 Cloud service models overview

  • The unit is the “as a service” stack: IaaS, then PaaS, then SaaS, then a comparison, then a few other names.
  • IaaS is cloud building the infrastructure blocks you still assemble.
  • PaaS is a platform you develop on, with the platform already running.
  • SaaS is the finished application.

014 Infrastructure as a service

  • IaaS gives the building blocks: networking (he includes routers, load balancers, switches, and a VPC), compute servers, and storage.
  • You do not buy the hardware. You do choose the size, the network, and what you install.
  • An EC2 instance is his running example of IaaS: a server you configure.
  • The provider runs the data center; you run the operating system upward.

015 PaaS

  • PaaS removes the need to manage the underlying network, compute, and storage.
  • He separates it from IaaS with EC2: on IaaS you create the instance and still patch and scale it; on PaaS the platform does that and you bring the application.
  • You deploy code. You do not pick the rack, the AMI, or the disk layout.
  • Elastic Beanstalk is the AWS name he is pointing toward, even if the product lecture comes later.

016 SaaS

  • SaaS is a finished application provided as a service.
  • There is no infrastructure to create, no deployment, and no production work on your side.
  • You sign in and use it. Gmail-style products are the picture, not an EC2 you SSH into.
  • If you are configuring servers, you are not in the SaaS seat for that system.

017 Tabular comparison

  • He puts IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS in columns and walks who manages each layer.
  • IaaS: you deploy and you still manage the stack above the hardware.
  • PaaS: the platform is deployed and managed; you manage the app.
  • SaaS: you manage nothing in the stack; you use the application.

018 Other services

  • He adds names beyond the three models. Code as a service: you describe infrastructure or tasks in Python, Java, or similar, and the platform creates them. He files Elastic Beanstalk here as well as with PaaS.
  • The idea is automation: the code is the request, and the provider performs the create, the API call, or the function.
  • He treats these extra labels as ways people extend IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS, not as a fourth required column on the exam table.
  • Remember the three models first; use this lecture when a slide says “other.”

What he is doing in this unit

Drawing who manages what. No console.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Place a raw virtual server in IaaS, a deploy-your-code platform in PaaS, and a finished app in SaaS.
  • Say what you still manage on EC2 that you do not manage on PaaS.
  • Read his comparison table from the bottom layer up.

07 — Cloud deployment models

019 Cloud deployment models overview

  • Deployment here means how the cloud is placed for a given organization, not how you ship an app.
  • The four he will cover are public, private, hybrid, and community.
  • They differ by who else shares the infrastructure and who can reach it.
  • Public comes first because it is the one a new AWS account actually is.

020 Public cloud

  • Services sit on a public network and anyone who can sign up may use them.
  • His examples are ordinary: a game in a browser, a social site, anything you open without owning the servers.
  • He calls it the cost-effective option for an individual because you share the provider’s estate.
  • AWS, as he has been using the word, is this model.

021 Private cloud

  • The infrastructure is dedicated to one organization, on its own premises or hosted somewhere else but not shared.
  • He ties it to the traditional IT unit: one company, one estate, tighter control.
  • The single organization is the line he uses to separate private cloud from community cloud.
  • You pay for isolation. You do not share the pool with strangers.

022 Hybrid cloud

  • Hybrid joins public and private so one organization can use both.
  • He lists flexibility and data sharing, and he adds security from the private side even when the slide omits it.
  • The catch he flags: you only get both benefits if you decide which data and which app live on which side.
  • A private database with a public website in front is the shape he is describing.

023 Community cloud

  • Infrastructure is shared by a specific community or a group of organizations, not by the whole internet and not by one company.
  • He points back at private cloud: private is one organization; community is several with a shared concern.
  • Members share cost and share a policy, such as a regulator or an industry.
  • Outsiders are not the audience, which is what keeps it out of the public column.

What he is doing in this unit

Defining four deployment models on slides. No console.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Match public, private, hybrid, and community to who shares the infrastructure.
  • Explain why he links private cloud to on-premises IT.
  • Describe hybrid as a deliberate split, not a mix of everything.

08 — Cloud computing challenges

024 Cloud computing challenges overview

  • The unit lists what still goes wrong after you leave the server room: security, vendor lock-in, monitoring, compliance, cost, migration, automation, and latency.
  • He sets them against AWS, Azure, and GCP, not against a toy example.
  • Each following lecture is one challenge and why it is hard.
  • Cost is the one he will illustrate from his own early bills.

025 Security

  • Public cloud means many customers on one provider, so “open the account and hope” is not a design.
  • The first job he names is protecting sensitive data that now lives in someone else’s data center.
  • You still choose who can reach it; the provider does not know your files.
  • He treats security as a standing challenge, not a feature you turn on once.

026 Vendor lock-in

  • Lock-in is depending on one provider because leaving is hard.
  • Moving an application and its data from Azure to AWS is not a copy: the app has to fit the new services, and the move takes time and money.
  • APIs, storage formats, and identity all differ, so a design that uses only one vendor’s extras is harder to leave.
  • He presents this as a planning problem, not as a reason to avoid cloud.

027 Monitoring

  • Monitoring is how you notice a threat or a failure; he calls it one of the pillars next to security.
  • Limited visibility is the challenge: a full picture is harder when the app spans two clouds or a hybrid link.
  • AWS and Azure each have their own monitors, and those tools do not automatically become one dashboard.
  • If you cannot see CPU, errors, and who changed a setting, you cannot say the system is healthy.

028 Compliance

  • Compliance is laws and rules, not a product setting.
  • Data residency: some countries require data to stay inside their borders, and the rules differ by country.
  • A provider has regions; you still have to pick a region the law allows and keep the evidence.
  • He frames this as “the data is easy to move, the statute is not.”

029 Cost management

  • Pay-as-you-go can spike. He describes learning-era bills from resources he left running.
  • Unpredictable expense is the challenge: the meter does not stop because you closed the laptop.
  • The fix he keeps repeating later is to delete labs and to notice what is still on.
  • This is the challenge he says hurts learners in particular.

030 Migration

  • Moving a large body of data securely is slow and easy to get wrong.
  • He scales the problem: gigabytes are small, terabytes are ordinary on fast networks, petabytes and beyond are where the move itself is the project.
  • You also have to move behavior, not just files: apps, cutover, and a window where both sides exist.
  • Migration is a challenge because the data is already in use while you copy it.

031 Automation

  • Automation fails when the scripts are wrong, and writing them takes a language plus a picture of the infrastructure.
  • You have to know what you are automating: a deploy, a create, or one repeated task.
  • You also have to know the service the script talks to, or the automation builds the wrong thing quickly.
  • He treats skill, not the existence of tools, as the hard part.

032 Performance and latency

  • Distance is the first cause: a user in Africa reading a US data center waits longer than a user next to that data center.
  • Latency is the extra time on the path; the application can be correct and still feel slow.
  • Putting a copy nearer the user is the remedy he is setting up for later edge and region lectures.
  • Performance here is about placement, not about buying a bigger single machine.

What he is doing in this unit

Naming eight challenges from slides, including his own surprise bills. No console.

After this unit you should be able to

  • List security, lock-in, monitoring, compliance, cost, migration, automation, and latency.
  • Explain lock-in as the cost of leaving, not as a fee on the invoice.
  • Connect a far region to a slow page.
  • Treat a forgotten running resource as a cost bug.

09 — Networking in cloud computing

033 Networking overview

  • The unit order is fundamentals, devices, protocols, designs, and then security.
  • He argues you cannot operate cloud if “network” is only a word, because the machines are not in the room.
  • Later AWS lectures reuse these names: subnet, load balancer, gateway, port.
  • This unit is the vocabulary, not the VPC console.

034 Networking fundamentals, part 1

  • You need a network because the infrastructure is in the provider’s data center and you cannot walk up to it.
  • Two requirements he repeats: the path has to be secure, and it has to be efficient.
  • Every later service still assumes a packet can get from you to that data center and back.
  • If the path is open to anyone, the remote server is open to anyone.

035 Networking fundamentals, part 2

  • Networking is connecting devices so they can share files, printers, and services such as the internet.
  • The one-word answer he wants: connection.
  • Subnetting is the first key feature he names: cutting a network into smaller ranges so you can say who sits where.
  • Addresses and ranges are how that cut is written down.

036 Networking devices

  • A router gives directions: it forwards traffic toward the network that contains the destination.
  • He walks the usual set around it: switches joining machines inside a network, and devices that balance or filter.
  • In cloud these devices are often services, but the job is the same as the box in a rack.
  • If you cannot say what a router decides, the later internet-gateway lecture will not stick.

037 Network protocols, part 1

  • A protocol is a formal procedure: rules for how data is formatted, sent, and received.
  • Without shared rules, two devices can be connected and still not understand each other.
  • He separates the definition from the catalogue; the examples are the next lecture.
  • Reliability, in his wording, is part of what the rules are for.

038 Network protocols, part 2

  • HTTP, hypertext transfer protocol, is the first concrete example, and he attaches a port to every protocol.
  • A port is a numbered door on a device for one kind of conversation.
  • He continues through the common set (secure HTTP, file transfer, remote login, name lookup) so a later security group has a number to allow.
  • Remember the pair: name of the protocol, number of the port.

039 Network design and architecture

  • Design is the planning pass: structure, components, layout, and how traffic flows.
  • His sketch: internet traffic enters an internet gateway, may pass an ELB, then reaches subnets in tiers across availability zones.
  • You draw this before you create it, because the path decides which tier is public.
  • The picture is the one the VPC unit will build for real.

040 Types of network architecture and best practices

  • Client-server: phones, laptops, and tablets are clients, and a server is what they all talk to so you do not manage every user as a special case.
  • He contrasts that with designs where machines are more equal, then returns to what cloud apps usually are: many clients, a few servers.
  • Best practice in this lecture is to pick the pattern on purpose and to keep the path short and documented.
  • A load balancer in front of those servers is the cloud version of “one front door.”

041 Network security

  • The reason is the data: personal data, financial records, and intellectual property such as private images and video.
  • He places that data in a private cloud environment or at least a private subnet, off the open internet.
  • Network security is which path is allowed, not a later add-on.
  • The same talk is repeated almost word for word in lecture 046; this is the first copy.

What he is doing in this unit

Defining devices, ports, and a traffic sketch. No AWS console yet.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Say why a cloud server still needs a network path.
  • Define a router, a subnet, a protocol, and a port.
  • Sketch gateway, then load balancer, then tiered subnets.
  • Give a reason to keep sensitive data off the public subnet.

10 — Security best practices

042 Security best practices overview

  • You reach cloud infrastructure over a network, usually the internet, so the path and the data on it have to be protected.
  • The unit will cover encryption, certificates, backup, network security, and account-level services.
  • He separates securing the pipe from surviving a loss.
  • This is still general practice; the IAM console is the next unit but one.

043 Encryption

  • Encryption turns human-readable plaintext into ciphertext that an outsider cannot read.
  • Algorithms do the conversion; he does not derive them, he only needs the two words.
  • The point is unauthorized access: stolen storage should not be readable text.
  • He will reuse plaintext and ciphertext when S3 and EBS encryption appear.

044 IAM

  • The file is named IAM, but the recording is AWS Certificate Manager, not users and policies.
  • ACM is the managed place to provision, store, and deploy SSL/TLS certificates for sites and applications on AWS.
  • It renews certificates so you are not buying and replacing them by hand from a third party.
  • He uses it for web apps, for API traffic, and for private certificates inside a company network. The real IAM service starts in unit 13.

045 Backup and high availability

  • He is explicit: backup and high availability do not stop an attack. If you are unencrypted and exposed, the attack still lands.
  • They protect the data after a loss and keep a service up when a piece fails.
  • A backup is a copy you can restore. High availability is a second path so one failure is not an outage.
  • He calls both core strategies for data protection, and he separates that from environment protection.

046 Network security

  • This recording repeats lecture 041: sensitive data is personal data, financial records, and intellectual property.
  • Keep it in a private environment or a private subnet so it is not sitting on the internet.
  • The security control is placement and access, not a new device introduced here.
  • Treat it as a second pass of the same argument, not as a new design.

047 Account management and security services

  • Providers also sell services whose only job is the account and its security, separate from servers and databases.
  • A learner with one account underestimates this; a company with many accounts cannot.
  • He points at dedicated monitoring and at tools that manage who is allowed in.
  • The later units (IAM, CloudTrail, organizations) are the AWS versions of this lecture.

What he is doing in this unit

Defining practices on slides. The certificate talk is spoken, not a console lab.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Define plaintext and ciphertext.
  • Say what ACM stores, even though this file is mislabeled IAM.
  • Separate backup from “security that blocks an attacker.”
  • Expect account-level tools once more than one person uses the account.

11 — Cloud providers

048 Cloud providers overview

  • He calls this the last basics section: you should know the providers the industry actually uses.
  • The set is AWS, Azure, GCP, IBM, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, Alibaba Cloud, and DigitalOcean.
  • For each he will give a launch era, a rough market position, and what they emphasize.
  • AWS is first because the rest of the course stays there.

049 AWS

  • Amazon Web Services is the cloud platform from the Amazon company: compute such as EC2, storage such as S3, databases such as RDS and Aurora, plus machine learning and security, over the internet.
  • You pay for what you use. More usage, higher bill.
  • He dates the public launch to 2006 and calls it the largest of the providers.
  • Lecture 057 repeats this same overview once the AWS half of the course starts.

050 Microsoft Azure

  • Azure is Microsoft’s cloud, launched in 2010, and he places it second, with a market share he quotes around 20 to 25 percent.
  • It offers the same kinds of models: IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS.
  • Global presence means many regions, each with several data centers.
  • He treats it as the main alternative when a company is already a Microsoft shop.

051 GCP

  • Google Cloud Platform, launched in 2008, is on-demand compute, storage, databases, networking, security, and machine learning, billed by use.
  • The specialty he wants remembered is machine learning.
  • He puts the market share near 10 percent and GCP in the top three with AWS and Azure.
  • The delivery model is the same shape as AWS; the emphasis is the difference.

052 IBM Cloud

  • He expands IBM as International Business Machines, then describes IBM Cloud as compute, storage, networking, databases, AI, and developer tools on a pay-as-you-go bill.
  • Launch is the early 2010s, four years after AWS, with a smaller niche share.
  • The catalogue looks like the big three; the market position does not.
  • Use it when a question is about the niche providers, not about the default lab account.

053 Oracle Cloud Infrastructure

  • OCI is Oracle’s platform: compute, storage, networking, databases, analytics, and security, with consumption-based pricing.
  • He dates the launch to 2016, later than AWS, Azure, and GCP.
  • The niche he names is enterprise and database-heavy work, which matches Oracle’s database history.
  • It is the provider he flags when the workload is “a lot of database,” not “a first Linux server.”

054 Alibaba Cloud

  • Alibaba Cloud is from Alibaba Group in China: compute, storage, networking, machine learning, security, and IoT, billed by use.
  • He defines IoT as a physical thing (he uses a bag, a guitar, a helmet) joined to a network and software so it becomes “smart.”
  • Founded in 2009, so it is an early platform even if it is not the one this course labs on.
  • Geography and IoT are the two marks he leaves on it.

055 DigitalOcean

  • DigitalOcean aims at developers, startups, and smaller businesses, and he says plainly it is not a big-three provider.
  • Launched in 2011. Virtual machines are called Droplets. It also offers storage, managed databases, Kubernetes, and app hosting, with straightforward pricing.
  • It has its own data centers and the same broad service models, at a smaller catalogue.
  • Droplet is the word to remember if a question refuses to say EC2.

What he is doing in this unit

Comparing providers on slides: launch years, share, and one specialty each. No console.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Name the seven providers in his list.
  • Give AWS 2006 and “largest,” Azure as second, GCP as the machine-learning emphasis.
  • Match OCI to databases, Alibaba to China and IoT, DigitalOcean Droplets to simple virtual machines.
  • Say the rest of the course stays on AWS.

12 — Introduction to AWS

056 Introduction to AWS overview

  • He sends you back to the account-creation lecture before this section if you do not have a console yet.
  • The plan is a short AWS recap, then history and future, a few facts, regions, and the shared responsibility split.
  • He tells you not to skip the region lecture even if the earlier ones feel familiar.
  • This is the doorway into the hands-on half.

057 AWS

  • The recording repeats lecture 049: AWS is Amazon’s cloud, with EC2 for compute, S3 for storage, RDS and Aurora for databases, plus machine learning and security.
  • Delivery is over the internet and the bill follows usage.
  • He again dates the launch to 2006 and calls AWS the widest of the platforms.
  • Use this as the recap at the start of the AWS half, not as new services.

058 AWS cloud history and future

  • He corrects the simple “launched in 2006” line: internally AWS was already being tested around 2002, and infrastructure build-out started in 2003.
  • 2006 is the public opening, after that internal period.
  • The future side of the lecture is more services on the same account, not a second cloud you have to relearn from zero.
  • Hold both dates: internal testing, then the public year.

059 AWS cloud facts

  • He quotes about $35 billion annual revenue in 2019 and more than $100 billion in 2024, nearly a triple.
  • He credits the jump to businesses moving infrastructure online during COVID-19.
  • The fact is scale: the platform you are about to click is the one those companies moved onto.
  • He uses the numbers to justify spending the rest of the course on AWS rather than on every provider equally.

060 AWS regions

  • He gives the count he is teaching from: on the order of 25 geographic regions and about 80 availability zones.
  • A region name is a code. US East 1 is the east coast of the United States; EU West 3 is a European region. The letters are the geography, the number is which site in that area.
  • A region contains several availability zones, which are separate data centers close enough to act as one region and far enough that one failure need not take the others.
  • You pick the region in the console before you create; a resource in one region is not automatically in another.

061 AWS shared responsibility model

  • The model answers who does what. He splits “security of the cloud” (AWS) from “security in the cloud” (you).
  • AWS runs the buildings, the hardware, and the managed service. You configure what you build: access, data, and settings.
  • If you leave a bucket open or an instance unpatched at the OS you control, that is the customer side.
  • He will repeat a version of this split at the end of many later units.

What he is doing in this unit

Recapping AWS on slides and decoding region names. No new resources created.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Separate the 2002 testing story from the 2006 public launch.

13 — IAM

062 IAM overview

  • IAM is Identity and Access Management. The unit order is users and groups, permissions, policies, the password policy, MFA, ways to sign in, roles, a few security reports, then best practice and the shared split.
  • He warns the shared-responsibility ending will show up at the end of later sections too.
  • Everything in the unit is about who may do what in the account you just created.
  • The root user is the account; IAM users are the people and roles are the services.

063 Users and groups

  • IAM in AWS is both the general idea and a global service: users are not stuck in one region.
  • The first login you created is the root user. He says not to use it or share it for daily work.
  • An IAM user is one identity. A group is a set of users who should get the same permissions.
  • You put people in groups so you are not attaching the same policy ten times.

064 Hands-on: IAM users and groups

  • Open IAM from the console search. The dashboard shows users, groups, roles, and policies he already has.
  • Users, Create user: he sets a name, enables console access, and sets a password.
  • He adds the user to a group (or creates the group) and attaches a policy so the user can do something besides sign in.
  • After create, the user exists in the account and can be given a sign-in link that is not the root email.

065 IAM permissions

  • A user or group without permissions can sign in and still not be allowed to touch resources.
  • Permissions are the list of allowed actions, and he says they are written as JSON documents because that form is readable enough to inspect.
  • You attach those documents to the identity. The identity does not “have power” by existing.
  • Least privilege is the direction: only the actions that job needs.

066 IAM policies

  • Identity-based policies attach to users, groups, or roles and say which actions those identities may take on which resources.
  • AWS managed policies are the pre-built ones you select. Customer managed policies are ones you write and reuse. Inline policies live on that one identity.
  • He contrasts those with resource-based policies, which sit on the resource (an S3 bucket is the later example) and name who may call it.
  • In the hands-on he has already attached managed policies; this lecture names the kinds.

067 Policy structure and password policy

  • He reads a policy as JSON: Version (he treats it as the date the policy was created), Id (a unique name), Statement, and Sid (statement id).
  • Inside the statement: Effect Allow, Principal the root ARN, Action such as s3:GetObject and s3:PutObject, Resource the example bucket he calls “my bucket.”
  • The account password policy is separate: minimum length, required character types, users may change their own password, rotation, and no reuse of recent passwords.
  • He sets that policy in the IAM account settings, not inside a single user.

068 MFA

  • Multi-factor authentication is a second check so a stolen password is not enough to change or delete resources.
  • He describes layers: something you know, then something you have. Two factors are enough for him; a third is possible.
  • It applies to root and to IAM users, not only to the email that opened the account.
  • Without MFA, the console password is a single door.

069 Access to AWS

  • Three ways in. The management console: browser, account id or user, password.
  • The command line, protected by an access key id and a secret access key, which he also compares to a key file such as a .pem when the target is an instance.
  • Programmatic access is the same keys used by scripts and SDKs, not a third kind of identity.
  • Console password and access keys are different credentials. You can have one without the other.

070 Hands-on: MFA and access keys

  • He opens the practice user from the previous lab and reads the summary: the ARN (he expands it as Amazon Resource Name), the user id, the create date, and an empty last-sign-in because the user is new.
  • Security credentials: he assigns an MFA device, scans the QR with an authenticator, and types two consecutive codes.
  • He then creates an access key for the CLI and tells you to save the secret, because the console shows it once.
  • Leave the lab with MFA on and with any unused key deleted.

071 IAM roles

  • A role is like a user that an AWS service assumes, not a person who types a password.
  • EC2 calling other services “on your behalf” is the example: the instance gets the role, and the permissions live on the role.
  • You do not put a long-lived access key on the server if a role can hand out temporary credentials.
  • Users have permissions for people. Roles have permissions for services and for cross-account trust.

072 Hands-on: IAM roles

  • Create role, trusted entity “AWS service,” and he picks EC2.
  • He attaches a predefined policy that lets the instance call other AWS services.
  • Name the role, create it, and it appears in the role list ready to be selected on a future instance.
  • The role does nothing until something assumes it. Creating it is not the same as attaching it.

073 IAM security tools

  • The credential report is account-wide: every user and the state of their passwords, keys, and MFA, downloaded as a file.
  • IAM Access Advisor is per user: which services that user is allowed to call, and when each was last used.
  • He uses the advisor to spot permissions nobody has touched.
  • Both are reports, not extra permissions.

074 Best practices and shared responsibility

  • Do not use root for daily work. If other people use the account, make yourself an IAM admin and give everyone else only what they need. If you are truly alone he allows root, and still prefers an IAM admin.
  • MFA on root, no sharing of keys, and remove users who have left.
  • AWS secures the IAM service itself. You secure who you create and which policies you attach.
  • The file title says “shared responsibility model for IAM”; the talk is mostly this best-practice list plus that split.

What he is doing in this unit

In IAM he creates a user, puts the user in a group, attaches a policy, turns on MFA, issues an access key, and creates an EC2 role. He also opens the password policy and the credential report.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Create an IAM user with console access and a group policy.
  • Read Effect, Action, and Resource in a small JSON policy.
  • Turn on MFA and tell a console password from an access key.
  • Create a role for EC2 instead of pasting keys onto a server.
  • Leave root unused for ordinary work.

14 — EC2

075 EC2 overview

  • EC2, Elastic Compute Cloud, is the virtual server service. The unit will launch one, then cover types, security groups, addresses, placement, network cards, hibernate, and how you pay.
  • He flags a shared-responsibility close and a CLI lecture at the end.
  • Almost every later lab starts from an instance, so this unit is the machine you keep rebuilding.
  • Elastic, in his usage, means the number and size can change.

076 EC2

  • An instance is a virtual machine: you pick an AMI (the starting image, such as Amazon Linux), an instance type (CPU and memory), storage, a key pair, and a security group.
  • You can start, stop, and terminate it. Stop keeps the disk and releases the compute; terminate deletes the instance.
  • It runs in one availability zone inside the region you selected.
  • He compares it to a computer you rent by the hour instead of a computer you buy.

077 Hands-on: first instance

  • EC2, Launch instance. Name it, pick Amazon Linux, and a free-tier size such as t2.micro or t3.micro.
  • Create a key pair and download the .pem. He says you cannot download it again.
  • The wizard’s security group allows SSH. He launches, waits until the state is running, and copies the public IP.
  • Connect with the key. Terminate the instance when the demo is over so the hour meter stops.

078 Instance types

  • The type name encodes a family and a size. He walks general purpose (the T family, including the free-tier micro), compute-optimized, memory-optimized, and storage-optimized.
  • A larger size in the same family is more vCPU and more RAM, and a higher price.
  • You match the family to the work: a balanced web server versus a memory-heavy database.
  • Changing type later means a stop, a change, and a start, not a checkbox on a running machine.

079 Hands-on: instance types

  • He launches again and opens the instance-type list so you can see the families and the free-tier badge.
  • He compares a micro with a larger type and points at the price difference on the same screen.
  • He does not leave the large type running. The point is to recognize the names.
  • Back on the instance page, the chosen type is visible in the details pane.

080 Security groups

  • A security group is a virtual firewall on the instance. Rules are allow-only; there is no deny rule.
  • You set protocol, port, and source. SSH 22 from your IP, HTTP 80 from anywhere, are the examples he uses.
  • It is stateful: if you allow the request in, the reply is allowed out.
  • A group can be attached to more than one instance, and an instance can have more than one group.

081 Hands-on: security groups

  • He edits inbound rules on the launch-wizard group: SSH, then HTTP, and he shows the source as “my IP” or 0.0.0.0/0.
  • Save rules. The change applies without rebooting the instance.
  • He tests that a closed port does not answer and an open one does.
  • He tells you not to leave SSH open to the world on a real server.

082 Security group properties

  • He lists behavior you cannot see from one rule: separate groups for different tiers, referencing another group as the source instead of an IP, and the default “allow all outbound.”
  • You can change a group at any time. The instance does not need a new launch.
  • Security groups do not span regions. A group in one region is invisible in another.
  • Locked-out SSH is fixed by adding a rule, not by rebooting.

083 Classic ports

  • The short list he wants memorized: 22 SSH, 21 FTP, 22-range for SFTP as he groups file transfer, 80 HTTP, 443 HTTPS, 3389 RDP for Windows.
  • Database ports come with the database unit; 3306 is the one he will use for MySQL.
  • A security group with no inbound rule allows no new connections in.
  • Knowing the port is what makes the next hands-on a choice instead of a guess.

084 Public, private, and Elastic IP

  • A private IP is how instances talk inside the VPC. It stays while the instance exists.
  • A public IP is for the internet and, on a normal instance, changes when you stop and start.
  • An Elastic IP is a public address you allocate and keep. He says you attach it so a restart does not change the address clients use.
  • An Elastic IP that is allocated and not attached is the one he warns can cost money.

085 Placement groups

  • A placement group is a hint about how close instances sit in the data center.
  • Cluster: close together, low latency, one availability zone, and they can fail together.
  • Spread: each instance on distinct hardware, for a small number of instances that must not fail together. Partition: spread into partitions so a large group loses only one slice.
  • You choose this at launch when the workload cares about either speed or failure, not for a single free-tier box.

086 Hands-on: placement groups

  • EC2, Placement groups, Create. He names one and picks a strategy (cluster, spread, or partition).
  • On the launch wizard he can then select that group so the new instance is placed in it.
  • He shows the group in the list and deletes it when it has no instances, because an empty group is only a label but the instances inside it are not free.
  • The console path is the whole lab.

087 ENIs

  • An ENI is an Elastic Network Interface: a virtual network card with its own private IP, public IP if you give it one, and security groups.
  • The primary ENI is created with the instance. You can add a second and move it to another instance.
  • He uses a second ENI when one machine must sit on two subnets or keep an address after the instance is replaced.
  • Security groups attach to the ENI, which is why changing the group changes the machine’s doors.

088 Hibernate

  • Stop throws away the contents of RAM. Hibernate writes RAM to the EBS root disk and later resumes the same processes.
  • He uses it for a long-running memory state you do not want to recompute, and he notes the root volume has to be large enough to hold that RAM.
  • Hibernate is not the same as terminate, and it is not available on every instance type.
  • You enable it before launch. It is not a button you discover after the fact on every AMI.

089 Hands-on: hibernate

  • In the launch wizard he turns hibernation on, which forces an encrypted root volume big enough for the memory.
  • After the instance is running he uses Instance state, Hibernate, waits, then starts it again.
  • The point of the demo is the menu path and the requirement, not a long-running app.
  • He terminates afterward.

090 Purchasing options

  • On-Demand: no commitment, you pay for what is running. This is the lab default.
  • Reserved and Savings Plans: a one- or three-year commit for a lower rate if you know the machine will stay. Spot: spare capacity, cheap, and AWS can take it back.
  • Dedicated hosts and dedicated instances are the isolated hardware options, at a higher price.
  • He spends this long lecture on when each is rational. A learning account stays on On-Demand.

091 Shared responsibility

  • AWS is responsible for the physical hosts, the hypervisor, and the network of the data center.
  • You are responsible for the guest operating system, the security group, the key pair, and the data you put on the instance.
  • Patching Amazon Linux is your job if you are the one who can SSH in. Patching the building is not.
  • He ties this back to lecture 061, now applied to EC2.

092 Hands-on: purchasing options

  • He opens the EC2 purchase screens for Reserved Instances, Savings Plans, and Spot requests so you can see the forms.
  • He does not buy. He says a reservation or a Spot request is a real charge and this course does not need one.
  • The lab is recognition: where the option lives, and which fields (term, instance family, max price) would commit you.
  • Close the wizard without submitting.

093 AWS CLI

  • The title says CLI. He actually opens CloudShell from the console toolbar, which is a shell already signed in as you.
  • He runs AWS CLI commands against S3: list buckets, make a bucket, upload a file, list objects.
  • CloudShell uses your console session, so he does not paste access keys into it.
  • He deletes the demo bucket before he leaves.

15 — EC2 instance storage

094 EC2 instance storage overview

  • The disk on the instance is the topic: EBS volumes, snapshots, AMIs, instance store, volume types, multi-attach, encryption, and EFS.
  • He separates “disk that survives stop” from “disk that is only local hardware.”
  • The hands-on lectures create extra volumes. He will tell you to delete them.
  • A shared-responsibility close restates who owns the bytes.

095 EBS volumes

  • EBS is Elastic Block Store: a network disk you attach to an instance, like a hard drive that is not inside the case.
  • It lives in one availability zone. You can detach it and attach it to another instance in that same zone.
  • The root volume is the one the operating system boots from. Extra volumes are for data.
  • Data stays when you stop the instance. Terminate can delete the volume if “delete on termination” is on.

096 Hands-on: EBS

  • EC2, Volumes, Create volume. He picks a size, gp2 or gp3, and the same availability zone as a running instance.
  • Actions, Attach, and he selects the instance and a device name.
  • On the instance he would format and mount it; the console part is create, attach, and later detach and delete.
  • An unattached volume still has a price. He deletes it.

097 Snapshots

  • A snapshot is a point-in-time copy of an EBS volume, stored in S3 even though you manage it from the EC2 snapshot page.
  • You can create a new volume from a snapshot, including in another availability zone, which is how a disk moves across zones.
  • Incremental: later snapshots store what changed.
  • He uses snapshots as backup, not as the live disk.

098 Hands-on: several EBS volumes

  • He creates more than one volume and attaches them to the same instance to show a machine can have several disks.
  • Each volume has its own id, size, and availability zone, listed on the instance’s storage tab.
  • He detaches the extras when the demo is done.
  • The root volume stays; pulling the root disk is not part of this lab.

099 Hands-on: snapshots

  • Select a volume, Actions, Create snapshot, and give it a description.
  • When the snapshot is completed he creates a volume from it and shows the new disk in the volume list.
  • He then deletes the snapshot and the extra volume.
  • The lab is the backup loop: volume, snapshot, volume again.

100 AMI

  • An AMI, Amazon Machine Image, is the template an instance boots from: operating system plus whatever you baked in.
  • You can make an AMI from a running instance, which uses snapshots of its volumes.
  • Launching from your AMI gives a new instance with that setup, in the regions where the AMI exists.
  • He distinguishes the image (reusable) from the instance (the running copy).

101 Instance store

  • Instance store is disk on the physical host, not EBS. It is fast and it is temporary.
  • Stop or terminate, or a host failure, and the data is gone. He limits it to cache and scratch.
  • Not every instance type offers it. You cannot detach it and move it.
  • If the data must survive a stop, it does not belong here.

102 Volume types

  • gp2 and gp3 are general-purpose SSD, the default for labs. io1 and io2 are provisioned IOPS for databases that need a guaranteed rate.
  • st1 is throughput HDD for large sequential files. sc1 is cold HDD, cheaper and slower.
  • He matches type to access pattern, not to “always pick the fastest.”
  • The free-tier root disk he uses is general purpose, small.

103 Multi-attach

  • Multi-attach lets certain io1 and io2 volumes attach to more than one instance in the same availability zone at once.
  • The instances must be in that zone, and the application has to tolerate a shared block device.
  • It is not a general file share. Two machines writing a normal filesystem without coordination will corrupt it.
  • He presents it as a special option, not the way you share a folder. EFS is the file-share lecture.

104 Encryption

  • You can encrypt an EBS volume at rest. He turns it on at create time with a KMS key, AWS-managed or one you make.
  • Snapshots of an encrypted volume are encrypted. Volumes made from those snapshots stay encrypted.
  • You do not get a button that encrypts a live unencrypted volume in place; the path is snapshot, copy encrypted, new volume.
  • The checkbox is your responsibility. The encryption machinery is AWS.

105 EFS

  • EFS is Elastic File System: a shared file system many Linux instances can mount at once, across availability zones.
  • It grows and shrinks with the files. You do not pick a fixed disk size the way you do with EBS.
  • Access is NFS. Security groups must allow the NFS port between the instances and the mount targets.
  • He uses it when more than one server must see the same files.

106 Hands-on: EFS

  • EFS, Create file system. Name it, pick the VPC, and let it create mount targets in the subnets.
  • The security group on the mount target must allow NFS from the instance security group.
  • On the instance he uses the mount command EFS shows you, then touches a file.
  • He deletes the file system afterward. EFS is not in the free tier the way a small EBS volume can be.

107 EFS versus EBS

  • EBS is one disk, one instance at a time (except the multi-attach case), one availability zone.
  • EFS is a shared folder, many instances, many zones in the region.
  • EBS is block storage you format. EFS is a file system you mount.
  • He picks EBS for a boot disk and a database disk, and EFS for files several web servers share.

108 Shared responsibility

  • AWS replicates EBS and EFS in the service, replaces failed hardware, and runs the encryption implementation.
  • You choose snapshot schedules, tick “encrypt this volume,” and you own the data on the drive.
  • Instance store is your risk: it is fast cache and it disappears when the instance stops.
  • Lectures 127, 192, 284, and 318 play this same storage split again under other section titles.

What he is doing in this unit

Creating and attaching EBS volumes, taking snapshots, restoring a volume, mounting EFS, and deleting the extras. He explains instance store, volume types, multi-attach, and encryption without leaving those bills running.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Attach an extra EBS volume in the same availability zone and delete it later.
  • Take a snapshot and make a volume from it.
  • Choose EBS, instance store, or EFS for a given file.
  • Say what “encrypt this volume” does and what a stop does to instance store.

16 — Scalability and load balancing

109 Scalability and load balancing overview

  • The unit separates three ideas he will define next: scaling, staying up, and spreading traffic.
  • Then the load balancer types, a comparison, two hands-on balancers, stickiness, connection draining, and auto scaling.
  • One recording in the middle is only the word “you,” and the closing “shared responsibility” file is the storage talk again.
  • The useful console work is the Application Load Balancer and the Auto Scaling Group.

110 Scalability

  • Scalability is the ability to handle more load by adding capacity.
  • He describes vertical scaling as a bigger instance (more CPU and RAM) and horizontal scaling as more instances.
  • Vertical hits a largest-size limit. Horizontal is how a web fleet grows.
  • He will later let an Auto Scaling Group do the horizontal part, and he sometimes also says the group “scales up” one machine. The definition in this lecture is the cleaner one: vertical is size, horizontal is count.

111 High availability

  • High availability means the service keeps answering when a piece fails.
  • He places instances in more than one availability zone so one data center is not the whole site.
  • A load balancer in front sends users to the instances that are still healthy.
  • Scalable is “can grow.” Highly available is “survives a failure.” He wants both words, not one word used twice.

112 Elasticity, scalability, and agility

  • Elasticity is automatic grow and shrink with the load, including shrinking when the spike ends.
  • Scalability is the capability to grow. Elasticity is that capability happening in response to demand.
  • Agility is how fast you can change the architecture, which he treats as a business outcome of not owning the hardware.
  • An Auto Scaling Group is his example of elasticity. Buying a bigger server once is only scalability.

113 Load balancing

  • A load balancer is the single address clients use. It spreads connections across instances.
  • He lists why: no single instance takes every user, and a dead instance can be skipped.
  • ELB is the AWS family name. The types are the next four lectures.
  • Health checks are how it knows a target is safe to use.

114 Application Load Balancer

  • An ALB works at the HTTP layer. It can choose a target group by path or host name.
  • Targets are instances, or IP addresses, in one or more availability zones.
  • He uses it for web applications, which is most of what this course builds.
  • Listeners are the ports the ALB itself opens, usually 80 and 443.

115 Network Load Balancer

  • An NLB works at the TCP and UDP layer and is built for very high connection rates and static IP addresses.
  • It does not inspect the URL. It forwards the stream.
  • He picks it when the protocol is not HTTP, or when the client must see a stable IP.
  • Health checks still decide which targets receive traffic.

116 Gateway Load Balancer

  • A Gateway Load Balancer sits in the path so traffic can be sent through virtual appliances such as firewalls you deploy.
  • It is not another web balancer. The targets are appliances, and the protocol he names is GENEVE.
  • You use it when every packet must be inspected by a third-party security device.
  • For a normal website he tells you to stay with the ALB.

117 Classic Load Balancer

  • The Classic Load Balancer is the old combined balancer, HTTP and TCP, from before ALB and NLB were split.
  • He mentions it so you recognize the name. New designs use ALB or NLB.
  • It still has listeners and health checks, with fewer routing features than an ALB.
  • If a console still offers “classic,” he treats it as legacy.

118 Load balancer comparison

  • ALB: HTTP and HTTPS, path and host rules, web apps.
  • NLB: TCP and UDP, high volume, static IPs.
  • Gateway: in-line appliances. Classic: the old one.
  • The comparison is the lecture he tells you to remember for exams.

119 Cross-zone load balancing

  • With cross-zone on, each load balancer node sends traffic to healthy targets in every availability zone, not only its own.
  • With it off, a zone with more instances does not automatically take a fair share from the other node.
  • He wants it on so users are even across zones.
  • ALB enables it by default in his telling; for NLB you should check.

120 Hands-on: Application Load Balancer

  • He launches more than one web instance, in more than one availability zone, with a security group that allows HTTP.
  • Create target group, instances, HTTP, and register those instances. Then create an ALB, internet-facing, pick the subnets, and point the listener at the target group.
  • He copies the ALB DNS name into a browser and refreshes to see it hit different instances.
  • He deletes the load balancer, the target group, and the instances at the end. An ALB is an hourly charge.

121 Hands-on: Network Load Balancer

  • He starts an NLB the same way: target group with TCP, then the NLB, subnets, and the listener.
  • The recording trails off before a full client test. The clicks he does complete are the target group and the balancer create path.
  • He contrasts the TCP health check with the HTTP check from the ALB lab.
  • Delete it if you follow along. He does not want this left running either.

122 Sticky sessions

  • Sticky sessions pin a user to one target, using a cookie, so the next request from that browser returns to the same instance.
  • He uses it when the application keeps session state in memory on one server.
  • The cost he names: one instance can get hot while others sit idle, and if that instance dies the session dies.
  • Turn it on only when the app requires it. A stateless app should not.

123 Connection draining

  • Connection draining, which the console also calls deregistration delay, is the time the load balancer waits after a target is removed so existing requests can finish.
  • New requests stop. In-flight requests get that window.
  • He sets it so a scale-in does not cut a user off mid-request.
  • Zero means drop immediately. A short number of seconds is his normal choice.

124 Auto Scaling Group

  • An ASG keeps a desired number of instances. If one fails a health check, it launches a replacement.
  • Minimum, desired, and maximum are the three numbers. A scaling policy changes desired when CPU or another metric crosses a line.
  • He attaches the group to a load balancer so new instances join the target group.
  • This is horizontal: more machines or fewer. When he also says the group upgrades one machine’s size, that is him mixing in vertical scaling. The group’s action in the console is count.

125 Note

  • The caption file contains only the word “you.”
  • There is no topic, no definition, and no console step in this recording.
  • Nothing from the Auto Scaling lecture should be assumed to live here.

126 Best practices

  • Pick the load balancer type for the traffic: ALB for HTTP, NLB for TCP, Gateway Load Balancer when appliances must see the packets.
  • Turn on cross-zone balancing, keep health checks on, and send users only to healthy targets.
  • Put an Auto Scaling Group behind the load balancer so a missing instance is replaced.
  • Terminate TLS at the load balancer, use sticky sessions only when a session forces it, set the idle timeout for long WebSocket connections, and leave Route 53 failover to the DNS unit.

127 Shared responsibility

  • The file sits in the load-balancing unit, but the audio is the storage responsibility talk from lecture 108.
  • AWS runs EBS and EFS replication and replaces failed disks. You configure snapshots and the encrypt checkbox, and you own the data.
  • Instance store is temporary cache and disappears when the instance stops.
  • There is no separate spoken split for the load balancer in this recording.

What he is doing in this unit

Building an Application Load Balancer in front of several instances, starting a Network Load Balancer, and describing an Auto Scaling Group, sticky sessions, and draining. He does not buy capacity. One file is empty of content, and the last file repeats the disk-responsibility talk.

  • Separate vertical scaling, horizontal scaling, and elasticity.
  • Choose ALB, NLB, or Gateway Load Balancer from the kind of traffic.
  • Create a target group and an internet-facing ALB, then delete them.
  • Set min, desired, and max on an Auto Scaling Group and say what a health check replacement does.

17 — S3

128 S3 overview

  • S3, Simple Storage Service, is the object store. The unit goes buckets and objects, policies, a static website, versioning, replication, storage classes, lifecycle rules, performance, batch jobs, and requester pays.
  • Hands-on lectures create a bucket and then delete it.
  • He will say storage is free and you pay to read, and later he will also show class prices. Each lecture is noted as he says it.
  • A bucket name is global. An object is the file inside it.

129 Buckets and objects

  • A bucket is the container. An object is the file plus its key (the path) and metadata.
  • He claims in this lecture that S3 storage itself is free and that you are charged when you retrieve. Later lectures discuss storage-class prices, so keep this as his statement here, not as the whole price list.
  • Objects are not a filesystem you edit in place. You upload a new object or delete one.
  • Durability is the property he stresses: the service keeps copies so a disk failure does not lose the object.

130 Hands-on: create, upload, delete

135 Versioning and replication

  • Versioning keeps older copies when you overwrite or delete. A delete without a version id adds a delete marker; the bytes can still be there.
  • Replication copies objects to another bucket, often in another region. Versioning must be on for replication.
  • He uses versioning as a way back from a bad upload, and replication as a second region.
  • Both are bucket settings, not a property you set on one upload only.

138 Storage classes

  • A lifecycle rule moves or expires objects after a number of days, so you do not change class by hand.

142 Performance and analytics

  • He talks about spreading keys so many uploads do not pile onto one partition, and about multipart upload for large files.

143 Batch operations

  • S3 Batch Operations runs one action across a list of objects: copy, tag, restore, or invoke a Lambda function.

144 Requester pays

  • Normally the bucket owner pays for requests and data transfer.
  • Requester Pays moves that charge to the account that downloads, which must send a special header and must be authenticated.

  • He uses it when you publish a large dataset and do not want to fund everyone else’s downloads.

146 Best practices

  • Block public access unless the bucket is a site you mean to publish. Then allow only GetObject.

  • Use a lifecycle rule instead of leaving every object in Standard forever.
  • Delete demo buckets. Unique names and leftover public policies are how student buckets show up in other people’s stories.

147 Shared responsibility

  • AWS runs the storage platform, the durability, and the service availability.
  • You choose the bucket policy, the public-access block, versioning, encryption, and the class.
  • AWS is not responsible for an object you made public or a file you deleted.
  • The split matches the earlier account model: they run S3, you run the contents and the permissions.

18 — S3 security

148 S3 security overview

  • This unit stays on one service: encryption, CORS, MFA delete, access logs, pre-signed URLs, object lock, and access points.
  • Several labs use CloudShell. He shows root access keys for MFA delete and says he will delete them.
  • He will also stop short of a retention lock he cannot undo.
  • Public access from the previous unit is the problem these lectures try to make precise.

149 Encryption types

  • Server-side encryption: S3 encrypts the object at rest. SSE-S3 uses keys S3 manages. SSE-KMS uses a KMS key you can audit. SSE-C is a key you send with each request.
  • Client-side encryption: you encrypt before upload, and S3 stores ciphertext it cannot read.
  • In transit he relies on HTTPS.
  • The default he wants on a new bucket is server-side encryption, usually SSE-S3 unless you need the KMS audit.

150 Hands-on: encryption

  • Bucket properties, default encryption. He turns it on and picks SSE-S3 or a KMS key.
  • New uploads then show the encryption on the object properties.
  • He does not demonstrate client-side encryption with a local tool in this lab.
  • He leaves default encryption on; it does not by itself make the bucket public or private.

151 CORS

  • CORS, cross-origin resource sharing, is the browser rule: a page on one site may not read a bucket on another origin unless the bucket says so.
  • You edit the CORS configuration with the allowed origins, methods, and headers.
  • This is not the same as a bucket policy. The policy decides the API call; CORS decides whether the browser will show the result to that page.
  • He uses it when a website loads fonts, images, or uploads directly to the bucket.

152 MFA delete

  • MFA delete, once versioning is on, requires a multi-factor code to permanently delete a version or to suspend versioning.
  • The console cannot turn it on. He says you need the CLI, and you need root credentials to do it.
  • A normal delete still creates a delete marker. Deleting the underlying version is what MFA blocks.
  • He treats this as protection against a stolen IAM key wiping history.

153 Hands-on: MFA delete

  • In CloudShell he runs aws configure with a named profile, root access key and secret, and region us-east-2, then aws s3 ls to prove the profile works.
  • He enables versioning with MFA using the root MFA device ARN and the current one-time code. Do not keep those root keys. He says he will delete them.
  • After it is on, deleting an object only adds a delete marker, and deleting the version is refused without MFA.
  • He disables MFA delete from the CLI. The first attempts error. After it is off he can permanently delete the version.

154 Access logs

  • Server access logging writes a record of requests against the bucket into another bucket.
  • He warns about the cost and about not logging a bucket into itself, which can loop.
  • There is no lab. He explains the setting and stops.
  • The destination bucket needs a policy that lets the logging service write.

155 Pre-signed URLs

  • A pre-signed URL is a normal object URL plus a signature and an expiry, so someone without an AWS account can download or upload until that time.
  • The signature is made with credentials that already have permission. The URL does not grant more than those credentials can do.
  • When the time is up, the same link fails.
  • He uses it for a private file you want to share once.

156 Hands-on: pre-signed URLs

  • He selects an object and uses the console or the CLI to create a pre-signed URL with a short lifetime.
  • He opens it in a browser and the file downloads even though the bucket is private.
  • He waits out, or he describes, the failure after expiry.
  • The object never became public. The link did.

157 Object lock

  • Object lock, on a versioned bucket, can make a version undeletable until a retain-until date, or until someone with a special permission uses governance mode.
  • Compliance mode cannot be shortened by anyone. Governance mode can, by the right IAM action.
  • A legal hold is a flag with no date.
  • He warns that a lock you cannot undo will also stop you deleting the bucket.

158 Hands-on: object lock

  • He enables object lock in the console UI and shows retention settings.
  • He does not save a retention period, so he can still delete the bucket.
  • The lab is the screen, not a locked object.
  • If you do save compliance retention, you are stuck with that bucket until the date.

159 Access points

  • An access point is a named entry to a bucket with its own policy, often limited to one VPC or one prefix.
  • Different teams can use different access points instead of one ever-growing bucket policy.
  • The bucket policy still has to allow the access point to delegate.
  • He presents this as a way to keep a shared data lake from having a single public switch.

160 Best practices and shared responsibility

  • Block public access, encrypt by default, version, and use least privilege in the bucket policy.
  • Prefer pre-signed URLs over a public object. Turn on MFA delete only if you can guard the root MFA device.
  • AWS runs S3. You run the policies, the keys, and the decision to lock or to publish.
  • Delete root access keys when a demo required them.

What he is doing in this unit

Turning on default encryption, explaining CORS and access logs without a full lab, enabling and then disabling MFA delete from CloudShell with root keys, creating a pre-signed URL, and opening object lock without saving a retention rule.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Pick SSE-S3, SSE-KMS, or client-side encryption for a case.
  • Say why a browser needs CORS even after the bucket policy allows the read.
  • Describe MFA delete as a CLI, root, versioning feature.
  • Mint a short-lived pre-signed URL for a private object.
  • Refuse to save a compliance lock on a practice bucket.

169 Databases and analytics overview

  • S3 and disks are unstructured. This unit is the services that let you query: RDS, Aurora, DynamoDB, Redshift, EMR, Athena, QuickSight, Neptune, QLDB, managed blockchain, Glue, Timestream, and DMS.

170 Relational databases

  • Files on EBS, EFS, instance store, or S3 are hard to query. A relational database looks like linked sheets.
  • SQL asks questions such as “name starts with A” or “age greater than 30.”
  • A primary key distinguishes two rows that share a name. His example is two students both called Jerry, separated by student id. A foreign key points at a row in another table.
  • RDS is where he will host PostgreSQL, MySQL, MariaDB, Oracle, SQL Server, and Aurora.

171 RDS

  • RDS is the managed database. Compared with MySQL you install on EC2, AWS patches the OS, takes backups you can restore to a time, shows metrics, and can add replicas.
  • His picture: users, then an ELB, then EC2, then RDS. The app queries; the database answers.
  • A read replica is an asynchronous copy, up to five on standard RDS, read-only, same zone or another region, with lag. Writes stay on the primary.
  • Multi-AZ is a synchronous standby and automatic failover, not a second place you write. A manual snapshot copied to another region is the disaster copy, and anything after the snapshot time is missing.

173 Aurora

  • Aurora is AWS’s engine that speaks MySQL or PostgreSQL. It is not a new query language. He claims about five times MySQL and three times PostgreSQL compared with those engines on plain RDS.
  • Storage grows from 10 GB toward 128 TB. He cites up to 15 read replicas and failover in seconds, with continuous backup to S3.
  • Provisioned Aurora: you pick the instance, storage scales. Aurora Serverless v2 scales compute in seconds. He says serverless still has servers; you do not manage them. Pause and resume is the older v1 behavior.

175 DynamoDB and DAX

  • DynamoDB is AWS’s managed NoSQL store: key-value or documents, items in one table need not share a shape, single-digit millisecond reads, and it scales horizontally.
  • The partition key identifies the item. An optional sort key lets several items share a partition key. The primary key is one or both.
  • He contrasts it with RDS: DynamoDB is active-active across replicas, not one primary plus a failover.
  • DAX, DynamoDB Accelerator, is an in-memory cache he says can take reads from milliseconds toward microseconds. It caches GetItem, Query, and Scan.

194 RDS Proxy

  • He draws three tiers: web, application, database. The database should query, but it also ends up authenticating and holding a connection per user.
  • RDS Proxy is a managed pool between the app (including Lambda) and RDS or Aurora. It keeps a smaller set of database connections and queues the callers.
  • It holds database credentials, can use Secrets Manager, enforces TLS, and he says failover is faster because the proxy already has the connections.
  • The database instance no longer spends its CPU on thousands of idle handshakes.

  • Global Database: one Aurora database replicated across regions for local reads and for failover. He is clear it is replicas, not one disk magically in every region.
  • Serverless v2: no cold start, millisecond scaling, works with existing clusters. He says v1 has cold starts and coarser scaling and is mainly a dev/test leftover.

  • A cache holds the data everyone asks for, in memory, so the database is not reread every time. His picture is a librarian keeping the popular books by the door.

  • Redis: several data types, snapshots, replication, pub/sub. Memcached: strings only, no snapshots, no replication, multi-threaded, simpler.
  • Redis when you need failover and richer structures. Memcached when you want a simple cache layer. He mentions Valkey as a newer option the console now offers.

22 — Containers and serverless

199 Containers and serverless overview

  • First half: Docker, then ECS, Fargate, and ECR. He calls ECS “Elastic Container Service” even when the caption says “plastic.”
  • Second half: what serverless means, Lambda, EKS, Lightsail, and API Gateway.
  • Several labs create clusters that bill by the hour. He deletes them on camera.
  • Kubernetes is the third-party orchestrator. ECS is the AWS one.

200 Docker

  • Docker is an open-source tool, not an AWS service. AWS can run what Docker builds.
  • A container is a small isolated package of code, libraries, and settings, so the app behaves the same on a laptop and on a server.
  • A Dockerfile is the script. An image is the snapshot built from it. A container is a running image. Docker Hub is the public library of images such as nginx and Python.
  • He contrasts this with one virtual machine per small app.

201 ECS

  • ECS schedules Docker containers on a cluster: when to start, stop, and scale them, plus CPU, memory, networking, and a load balancer.
  • Fargate launch type: AWS runs the machines, you pay per task, and he says it costs more because you are not managing servers. EC2 launch type: you manage the instances and it can be cheaper at scale.
  • He prefers ECS when you are already on AWS and do not want to learn Kubernetes. EKS is the AWS way to run Kubernetes if you do.
  • IAM, CloudWatch, and an ALB are the integrations he lists.

203 Fargate

  • Fargate is the serverless compute for containers. It works with both ECS and EKS.
  • You do not provision EC2, patch it, or attach a scaling group. Fargate adds tasks as demand grows and you pay for CPU and memory per second.
  • Each task is isolated. CloudWatch, IAM, and load balancers still attach.
  • He describes it as ECS with the machines taken off your list.

205 ECR

  • ECR is Elastic Container Registry: a private Docker registry on AWS, which he calls AWS’s Docker Hub.
  • You push images from your machine or from a pipeline, and ECS, Fargate, or EKS pull them.
  • IAM controls who pushes and pulls. He also mentions built-in vulnerability scanning and image tags.
  • Use it when the image was built for AWS. Docker Hub still exists for public images such as the nginx he already ran.

206 Serverless

  • Serverless means you do not provision, scale, or patch servers. The servers still exist; the provider runs them.
  • Functions run when an event happens. His example: a new CSV in S3 triggers a function that processes that file.
  • You pay for the run, and the capacity moves with the number of events.
  • His sketch is you, then Lambda, then S3 or a database, and the function does the processing you did not stand up a server to do.

207 Lambda

  • You upload code. Lambda runs it when an API, an S3 upload, a database change, EventBridge, SNS, or SQS triggers it, and you pay only while it runs.
  • He says a function runs at most 10 minutes, and that this limit is an exam point. Languages he names include Python, Node.js, Java, and Go.
  • The function needs an execution role before it may call S3, EC2, or RDS.
  • Free tier as he states it: 1 million requests and 400,000 GB-seconds a month, then a small per-request and per-GB-second price. Uses he lists: resize an image, send email with SES, scheduled jobs, and a small web back end.

23 — DNS and Route 53

217 DNS and Route 53 overview

  • DNS is how a name such as www.example.com becomes an address the browser can open.
  • The unit is DNS itself, then Route 53, record types, TTL, routing policies, a console tour, and the responsibility split.
  • He says the routing policies matter for exams.
  • You do not need to buy a domain to follow the hosted-zone screens.

218 DNS

  • He calls DNS the phone book of the internet. Browsers understand IP addresses, not names.
  • He splits a name into the top-level domain (.com, .in), the second-level name (the site), and the host such as www. The full string is the fully qualified name.
  • His lookup path: browser, local DNS, root server, top-level-domain server, then the authoritative server that returns the address.
  • The recording’s diagram narration is messy. The hierarchy root, then TLD, then the site’s own server is the part to keep.

219 Route 53

  • Route 53 is AWS’s DNS. He also calls it a domain registrar (the book of names and addresses you can register) and a health checker.
  • A health check answers whether an endpoint is up, the same idea as an ELB health check, so DNS can stop offering a dead address.
  • He says it is the AWS service with a 100 percent availability commitment in its service-level agreement.
  • Each record has a name, a type, a value, a routing policy, and a TTL. He saves the types and the policies for the next lectures and tells you not to skip the policies.

220 Record types

  • An A record maps a name to an IPv4 address, such as an EC2 instance or a load balancer.
  • An AAAA record (he says “quad A”) maps a name to IPv6. He says IPv4 is still the one you meet most often.
  • A CNAME makes one name an alias of another, and he limits that use to subdomains, such as www pointing at the apex name.
  • An NS record lists the name servers for the domain. A new Route 53 hosted zone gives you NS records to copy to the registrar so Route 53 becomes authoritative.

221 TTL

  • TTL, time to live, is how long a resolver may cache the answer Route 53 gave it.
  • High TTL, his example 24 hours: fewer queries, lower Route 53 cost, and stale answers stick around. He uses it when the target does not change and traffic is modest.
  • Low TTL: resolvers ask again quickly, which he says costs more because Route 53 answers more queries, and instances see more new connections. A login that dies in minutes is his everyday picture of a short TTL.
  • Exam line he wants: low TTL is the expensive one.

222 Routing policies

  • These policies answer DNS queries. They are not the load balancer. He corrects himself when he says “traffic.”
  • Simple: one record, or several values of which the client picks one at random. No health check. Weighted: you set weights and the share is that weight over the sum. Latency: send the name to the region with the lowest latency for that user. Failover: primary, then secondary if the health check fails.
  • Geolocation is the user’s country or continent, even if another region would be faster, and he says to keep a default record. Geoproximity uses a bias and needs Traffic Flow. IP-based uses a list of CIDR blocks. Multi-value returns several healthy answers so the client can choose.
  • He tells you to memorize the list more than the arithmetic.

223 Hands-on: Route 53

  • Hosted zones, create, a practice name such as example.com, public hosted zone. Private would bind the zone to a VPC; he does not want that yet.
  • Create record. He shows A records, the alias toggle that can point at an AWS resource such as a load balancer or Global Accelerator, and then each routing policy.
  • Weighted asks for a weight and an optional health check. Simple has no health check. Failover makes the health check required. Geolocation picks a country or continent. Latency picks a region such as Singapore. IP-based wants a CIDR collection. Geoproximity is a map coordinate, and he also shows local-zone names versus region codes.
  • He does not build a live site here. He says a later project would wire Route 53 to other services. Creating the public zone is the part that really happens.

224 Best practices and shared responsibility

  • Use alias records for AWS resources, turn on health checks for failover, and pick TTL knowing it changes both the DNS bill and how fast a change is seen.
  • Least privilege on who can edit the zone, and use more than one routing policy only when the design needs it.
  • AWS runs the service, the query handling, and the DDoS protection he says comes with it (he calls the attack a “dedicated denial of service”; the security unit will define it properly). You choose records, TTL, health checks, and whether you register the domain at Route 53.
  • If Route 53 is the registrar, AWS keeps the registration; renewing and the contact settings are still yours.

What he is doing in this unit

Creating a public hosted zone and opening the record form for every routing policy. He does not buy a domain or attach a live server. Health checks are discussed and, for failover, shown as required.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Walk a name from the local resolver to an authoritative answer.
  • Write an A record, an AAAA record, a CNAME, and an NS record in one sentence each.
  • Say why a low TTL costs more.
  • Pick simple, weighted, latency, failover, or geolocation for a scenario he described.
  • Create a public hosted zone and find the policy list.

24 — Global services

225 Global services overview

  • CloudFront, Global Accelerator, Outposts, Local Zones, S3 CORS, and Wavelength, then a comparison he says exams ask.
  • The shared theme is putting work nearer the user without copying the whole region.
  • S3 CORS is the odd one: it is a bucket setting, filed here because a global site trips over it.
  • He will show one CloudFront distribution he already owns and will create, then delete, an accelerator.

226 CloudFront

  • CloudFront is the content delivery network. It caches content at edge locations near the viewer so the origin is not hit for every request.
  • Origins he names: S3, EC2, or a server that is not on AWS. The edge holds a cache, not the whole database. He says you might cache a small fraction, not a giant store.
  • Invalidation removes an object from the edge before the cache time is up.
  • The viewer talks to CloudFront. CloudFront talks to the nearby cache or back to the origin.

227 Global Accelerator

  • Global Accelerator sends user traffic onto the AWS backbone instead of the public internet, for applications that have users in many regions.
  • You get static IP addresses as the front door. Anycast routing lands the user at a nearby edge, then the backbone carries them to an EC2 instance, an ALB, or an NLB.
  • Health checks move traffic off a dead endpoint. Traffic dials and endpoint weights let you shift a percentage, which he compares to weighted DNS.
  • It accelerates the path. It does not cache the body of a web page.

228 CloudFront versus Global Accelerator

  • CloudFront is a cache for HTTP and HTTPS: sites, media, APIs. Global Accelerator is a path for TCP and UDP, and he also allows HTTPS when the case is gaming or a raw application endpoint.
  • CloudFront caches. Global Accelerator does not. It routes and speeds up.
  • He pairs Route 53 with CloudFront often. Global Accelerator gives you the static IPs and performs the endpoint health checks. He says CloudFront does not health-check your origin the way the accelerator does.
  • Exam line he repeats: CDN and HTTP means CloudFront. TCP or UDP acceleration means Global Accelerator.

231 Local Zones

  • A Local Zone is a small extension of a region, placed near a dense city, still operated by AWS. His example is Los Angeles attached to US West 2.
  • You can run EC2, EBS, ECS, EKS, ELB, and RDS there. He quotes latency under about 10 milliseconds for users in that city.
  • He separates this from an edge cache: a Local Zone is a place you launch compute, usually in an industry hub, not a CloudFront point of presence.
  • Uses he names: media rendering, machine-learning inference, and trading.

232 S3 CORS

  • Cross-origin resource sharing lets a page on one domain read objects in a bucket that lives on another. His example is www.x.com calling another.com.
  • Console path: bucket, Permissions, Cross-origin resource sharing, edit, paste the allowed origin, save.
  • The browser sends the origin. The bucket must answer with a matching CORS header or the browser hides the response. He notes the “server” in the diagram is AWS storage, not an EC2 instance you built.
  • Allow one origin or any origin. He uses this for images, video, and fonts during development, including localhost.

233 AWS Wavelength

  • Wavelength places compute at the edge of a telecom provider’s 5G network so a phone reaches AWS without crossing the public internet first.
  • A Wavelength Zone is an extension of a region, like a Local Zone, but the users are mobile devices on that carrier. He again talks about latency around 10 milliseconds or less.
  • The picture: region, high-bandwidth link, Wavelength Zone, the carrier’s towers, then phones, vehicles, and headsets.
  • Do not swap the name with Local Zones. Local Zones are city extensions. Wavelength is the mobile-network extension.

235 CodeArtifact, CodeGuru, and CodeStar

  • CodeArtifact stores build packages. A developer or a CI system publishes into it and the pipeline pulls from it, instead of fetching every dependency from the public internet each time.

236 CloudFormation

  • CloudFormation builds AWS resources from a template in YAML or JSON: how many instances, which load balancer, which type, whether a database is attached.
  • A stack is the set of resources one template created. You can reuse the template and keep versions of it.
  • It creates dependencies in order, rolls back if a step fails, and a change set shows the diff before you apply it.
  • His flow: write the template, upload it or store it in S3, create the stack, watch the resources appear, update or delete the stack later. Monitoring afterward is CloudWatch, or Prometheus and Grafana if you add them.

237 Pipeline services

  • Cloud9 is the browser IDE: edit, run, and debug without a local install, with a terminal and the AWS CLI.
  • CodeCommit is private Git hosting. CodeBuild compiles and tests in an isolated environment. CodeDeploy rolls a version onto EC2, Lambda, ECS, or your own machines, including blue/green and rolling styles he names but does not expand.
  • CodePipeline chains them: source, build, test, optional approval, deploy. A trigger can be a schedule or a failure. It also connects to GitHub and Jenkins.
  • His diagram: Cloud9 or CodeCommit as source, CodeBuild as build and test, CodeDeploy as deploy, and CodePipeline as the thing that orders those steps. A failed build does not deploy.

238 DevOps

  • DevOps is development plus operations: build the software and keep running it, as one collaboration rather than a handoff.
  • CI/CD is continuous integration and continuous deployment: change the code and roll the change out, instead of rebuilding the product from nothing.
  • Automation covers tests, deploys, and infrastructure as code. Monitoring is CloudWatch or, third party, Grafana and Prometheus. Collaboration tools he names are CodeCommit and GitHub.
  • His loop is plan, write infrastructure (CloudFormation, Terraform, Kubernetes, ECS), build with Jenkins or the AWS tools, deploy, operate, and feed metrics back into the next change.

239 Elastic Beanstalk

  • Elastic Beanstalk is PaaS. You upload the application and it provisions EC2, a load balancer, and auto scaling.

  • FSx for Windows File Server speaks SMB and NTFS, for Windows apps that should keep their file server and store the bytes on AWS.
  • FSx for Lustre is the fast file system for high-performance computing, machine learning, and media. He reads Lustre as Linux plus cluster, and POSIX as the portable interface Linux uses. It ties to S3.
  • FSx for NetApp ONTAP runs that company’s file system in AWS: NFS, SMB, iSCSI, snapshots, dedup, and a copy-out to S3.
  • FSx for OpenZFS is the Linux file system that is also a volume manager, with snapshots and a CLI. CloudWatch is the monitor he attaches.

245 Hands-on: FSx

  • He creates FSx for Lustre: a practice name, persistent SSD, 125 MB/s per terabyte, automatic metadata, default VPC and security group. He does create this one. It comes out at about 1.2 TB, which is why it is slow and why he tells you to delete it.

246 Storage Gateway

  • Storage Gateway lets an on-premises application use cloud storage with a protocol it already speaks. You run the gateway as a VM or on EC2, and it caches hot files locally.
  • S3 File Gateway: NFS or SMB in, objects in S3. Volume Gateway: iSCSI block volumes, stored in S3, with EBS snapshots as the backup. Cached volumes keep hot blocks local. Stored volumes keep the full volume local and snapshot to AWS.
  • Tape Gateway pretends to be a tape library so existing backup software can write to S3 and Glacier without a change. He says this is how you retire physical tapes.
  • FSx File Gateway presents an FSx for Windows file share over SMB to a branch, with a local cache. The hands-on will show this type is closed to new customers.

248 Transfer Family

  • Transfer Family is managed SFTP, FTPS, and FTP directly into S3 or EFS. He is strict: not EBS, not RDS, only those two.
  • FTP is the plain protocol, SFTP is SSH file transfer, FTPS is FTP with TLS. He does not dwell on the cryptographic difference.
  • Uses he names: partners uploading files, and moving off an old on-premises FTP server.
  • Identity can be the service directory or your own. The files land as objects or as EFS files.

249 DataSync

  • DataSync copies data quickly, encrypted in transit, between on-premises storage and S3, EFS, or FSx, and also between AWS regions and accounts.
  • He says it is on the order of ten times faster than a naive copy, and that the speed is why it costs more than a casual sync.
  • Uses: fill S3 for machine learning, replicate for disaster recovery, and move a bucket between regions.
  • It is a transfer service. It is not a file system you mount for daily edits.

250 App2Container

  • App2Container is a command-line tool that turns a Java or .NET application running on a virtual machine or a bare-metal server into a container.
  • The source can be on EC2 or in your own building. The output is an image and the bits you need to run it on ECS or EKS.
  • He flags the exam phrase: a CLI tool that containerizes an app already running on a server.

252 Monitoring overview

  • CloudWatch for metrics and logs, EventBridge for events, CloudTrail for the audit of API calls, X-Ray for traces, and the Health Dashboard for AWS’s own status.

253 CloudWatch

  • CloudWatch collects metrics, logs, and events from EC2, Lambda, RDS, and from applications if you send them.
  • A metric is a number over time, such as CPU. An alarm fires when the number crosses a threshold and can notify SNS or trigger scaling.
  • Dashboards graph the metrics. Logs Insights queries log lines. The CloudWatch agent on an instance collects memory and disk, which the hypervisor does not see by itself.
  • EventBridge is the bus he says CloudWatch events feed. He treats CloudWatch as the place you look, and the alarm as the thing that acts.

258 X-Ray

  • A service map shows the calls. Segments break one trace into timed steps.

28 — Decoupling

262 Decoupling overview

  • The services are SQS, SNS, Kinesis (he often says “kindness”), Amazon MQ, and a best-practices file.
  • Decoupling means one component can fail or slow down without taking the next component with it.
  • Queues, topics, and streams are three different ways to pass a message.
  • The only real console lab is a short SNS topic.

263 Decoupling

  • Coupling is when component A and component B depend on each other’s internals. If A stops, B stops.
  • Decoupling separates them enough that B keeps working when A fails.
  • He lists the results: scale one piece without scaling the other, replace one piece without a rewrite, and simpler maintenance.
  • The rest of the unit is the AWS services that sit between those pieces.

264 SQS

  • SQS is Simple Queue Service, a managed queue. Producers send messages. Consumers pull them when they are ready. His picture is ten requests, two being processed, eight waiting, so EC2 is not overrun.
  • A visibility timeout hides a message while a consumer works on it, so a second worker does not take it too. A dead-letter queue keeps messages that keep failing.
  • Standard queues: high throughput, at-least-once delivery, no strict order. FIFO queues: first in first out, and exactly-once processing, at lower throughput.
  • Producers and consumers in his examples are applications on EC2 or Lambda, not people watching a screen.

265 SNS

  • SNS is Simple Notification Service, publish and subscribe. A publisher sends to a topic. Every subscriber receives a copy.
  • Publishers he names: your application, Lambda, CloudWatch alarms. Subscribers: email, SMS, Lambda, SQS, or an HTTP endpoint.
  • Fan-out means one message reaches many subscribers. He uses the fan as the picture.
  • Two services that barely know each other can still hear about the same event by sharing a topic.

266 Hands-on: SNS

  • SNS, create topic, standard, a name such as an S3 bucket topic. He creates it.
  • He opens a bucket, properties, event notifications, event type all object-create events, destination the SNS topic, and he can paste the topic ARN.
  • The recording is short and stops around that form. It does not show a test upload and a received email.
  • The clicks that are there: topic first, then the bucket event pointed at the topic.

267 Kinesis

  • Kinesis ingests a stream: logs, metrics, clickstreams, IoT signals, and he also says video.
  • Producers put records into a stream. Shards are the capacity slices. Consumers he names are Lambda, Kinesis Data Analytics, or your own app, and you can land the result in S3 or Redshift.
  • The four products, which the next lecture splits: Data Streams, Firehose, Data Analytics, and Video Streams.
  • Exam hint he gives: if the question says streaming, think Kinesis.

268 Kinesis types

  • Firehose loads a stream into S3, Redshift, OpenSearch, or Splunk, and can call Lambda to transform records. You do not manage shards. Latency is seconds, because it buffers.
  • Data Analytics runs SQL or Apache Flink on the stream. Uses he names: fraud, sensor aggregates, live metrics.
  • Video Streams takes camera and audio feeds and can pass them to Rekognition. Data Streams is the base product: you manage shards, consumers pull, retention up to seven days.
  • His comparison: Data Streams is manual and custom, Firehose is the easy delivery, Analytics is the only one that queries, Video Streams is the media one. Data Streams is the one that is not serverless in his telling.

269 Amazon MQ

  • Amazon MQ is a managed broker for Apache ActiveMQ and RabbitMQ, using protocols he lists: JMS, AMQP, MQTT, STOMP, OpenWire.
  • He recommends it when an existing application already speaks those protocols. Otherwise SQS or SNS is the native choice.
  • Exam line he wants: ActiveMQ or RabbitMQ in the question means Amazon MQ.
  • Uses: migrate a legacy broker, integrate with on-premises, and keep asynchronous delivery with messages that persist.

270 Best practices

  • The recording is the load-balancing checklist, not queue advice.
  • Right ELB type, cross-zone, health checks, Auto Scaling Group, TLS at the balancer, sticky sessions only when needed, idle timeout, and Route 53 deferred.
  • For the services in this unit, the practice he stated earlier is to pick a standard queue, a FIFO queue, a topic, or a stream on purpose.
  • Do not leave a Kinesis shard or an MQ broker running after a demo. This lecture does not create either.

What he is doing in this unit

Creating a standard SNS topic and pointing an S3 object-create event at it. SQS, Kinesis, and MQ are explained without a finished lab.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Define coupling as shared fate, and name a queue as one way to break it.
  • Contrast a standard queue with a FIFO queue, and a queue with an SNS fan-out.
  • Place Firehose, Data Analytics, Video Streams, and Data Streams.
  • Say Amazon MQ is for ActiveMQ and RabbitMQ, not for a new cloud-native app.

29 — Networking in AWS

271 Networking in AWS overview

  • The build order: a VPC, subnets, an internet gateway, a NAT gateway, flow logs, peering, endpoints, and a transit gateway.
  • He has been clicking “default VPC” since the EC2 unit. This unit makes a second one on purpose.
  • The last file is the storage responsibility recording again.
  • NAT is described and not left running, because he says it costs money.

272 VPC

  • A VPC is your isolated network in a region. He compares it with an on-premises data center, and elsewhere with a private network boundary. It is not a VPN product. A VPN would be a tunnel into it.
  • Inside it: subnets, route tables, and gateways. EC2, RDS, and Lambda can be launched into it, which is why every earlier wizard asked for a VPC.
  • The address range is a CIDR block you choose, and it must not overlap with another network you will connect.
  • One region can hold many VPCs. The default VPC is the one AWS made so the first instance had somewhere to live.

273 Hands-on: VPC

  • VPC, create, VPC only. He names it demo prac example 1, chooses manual IPv4, and uses 10.0.0.0/24. He declines an IPv6 block.
  • Other settings stay default. He creates it and reads the id, the CIDR, and the owner.
  • There are no subnets yet, and no flow logs. He says those are the next labs.
  • A VPC with no subnet still cannot hold an instance. The next lecture adds one.

274 VPC and subnets

  • Subnets slice the VPC so you can decide which resources have a route to the internet.
  • A public subnet has a route to an internet gateway. A private subnet does not. Private traffic that must leave goes through a NAT gateway sitting in a public subnet.
  • He puts web servers in public subnets and databases in private ones. Anything after the NAT in his diagram is private.
  • Each subnet sits in one availability zone. Two zones means at least two subnets.

275 Hands-on: subnets

  • Subnets, create, select the demo VPC, name subnet 1, no availability-zone preference, and a CIDR inside the VPC range.
  • He then creates a route table, names it, associates it with the same VPC, and attaches it to the subnet.
  • Without a route to an internet gateway the subnet is private even if you meant it to be public. He has not added that route yet.
  • Flow logs are mentioned and deferred.

276 VPC flow logs

  • Flow logs record IP traffic to and from network interfaces in the VPC: accepted, rejected, or both, with timestamps.
  • He uses them for troubleshooting and for security review, and later security tools can read the same logs.
  • Destinations: an S3 bucket or a CloudWatch log group.
  • The log does not block traffic. It only writes down what the security group and the route already did.

277 Hands-on: flow logs

  • On the demo VPC he creates a flow log, all traffic, aggregation interval 10 minutes, and a destination in S3 or CloudWatch.
  • The log appears on the VPC’s flow-logs tab. He notes a moment where the console says it is not available yet.
  • He also points at network ACLs, CIDR reservations, and tags, and does not configure them.
  • Creating the log is the lab. Reading a busy log is not, because the VPC has no traffic.

278 Internet gateway and NAT gateway

  • An internet gateway is the horizontally scaled VPC component that lets a public subnet talk to the internet. One per VPC.
  • A NAT gateway lets instances in a private subnet open outbound connections, and it does not allow the internet to start a connection in. He places the NAT in a public subnet.
  • Databases stay private. A less important app server may be public. That is the rule he repeats.
  • The internet gateway is free to attach. The NAT gateway is an hourly service, which is why the lab avoids leaving one up.

279 Hands-on: gateways

  • Create internet gateway, name it, attach it to the demo VPC. The list only offers VPCs that do not already have one.
  • He shows the attachment on the VPC page. A route in the public subnet’s table toward the gateway is what makes the subnet public.
  • He explains NAT creation: allocate it in a public subnet, then point the private route table at it. He recommends you delete it immediately if you create one, and he would rather you did not.
  • The internet gateway is the piece he actually attaches.

280 VPC peering

  • Peering connects two VPCs so they use private IP addresses as if they were one network, and the traffic does not go over the public internet.
  • His limit: two VPCs per peering connection. More VPCs means a mesh of peerings, which he says gets messy.
  • The CIDR blocks must not overlap. Routes must be added on both sides. Peering is not transitive: A peered to B and B peered to C does not let A reach C.
  • It can cross accounts and regions. The lab uses one account and one region.

281 Hands-on: VPC peering

  • He creates a second VPC with a different CIDR.
  • Peering connections, create, name, requester is the demo VPC, same account, same region (US East 1), acceptor is VPC 2.
  • The connection shows requester and acceptor. He stops at create. Accepting it, if the console still says pending, and adding routes both ways, is what makes packets flow. The recording shows the connection established at create time in his account.
  • Two empty VPCs are enough for him to call the lab done.

282 Transit Gateway

  • A transit gateway is a hub. Many VPCs and an on-premises network attach to it, instead of peering every pair.
  • He uses peering for exactly two VPCs and the transit gateway when the mesh would be larger.
  • Each attachment has a route. The hub is transitive if you design the tables that way, which peering is not.
  • His diagram is three AWS VPCs plus one on-premises network on the same hub. He does not create one. Attachments are billed.

283 VPC endpoints

  • A VPC endpoint is a private path from the VPC to an AWS service, so the traffic does not go out to the public internet and back.
  • His analogy is a train that skips the intermediate stations. The service, such as S3, is the destination.
  • Gateway endpoints and interface endpoints are the two kinds the console offers. He does not build either in a lab.
  • He mentions the endpoint as a way to avoid NAT data-processing charges for service traffic.

284 Shared responsibility

  • The audio is the EBS and EFS responsibility talk again, not a VPC-specific split.
  • AWS replicates the disks and replaces hardware. You configure snapshots and encryption and you own the data. Instance store is temporary.
  • For the network you just built, the same pattern is: AWS runs the physical network, you design the CIDR, the routes, and the security groups.
  • Do not treat this file as new VPC content.

What he is doing in this unit

Creating a VPC with 10.0.0.0/24, a subnet, and a route table. Adding a flow log. Attaching an internet gateway. Creating a second VPC and a peering connection. He describes NAT, transit gateway, and endpoints without leaving them running.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Create a VPC, a subnet, and a route table, and say what makes the subnet public.
  • Attach an internet gateway and explain why a NAT gateway is not the same door.
  • Peer two VPCs whose CIDR blocks do not overlap.
  • Choose peering for two VPCs and a transit gateway for a larger set.
  • Say a flow log records traffic and does not filter it.

301 DDoS, Shield, and WAF

  • He launches a small instance with an existing key pair so there is something to analyze.
  • In Firewall Manager he opens the Shield network-security director and starts a network analysis. It reports on security groups and network ACLs and a severity.
  • He compares Shield Standard with Shield Advanced and does not subscribe to Advanced, because there is no production app and Advanced is the costly one.
  • Terminate the instance afterward. The analysis was the point.

303 Hands-on: WAF

  • From Firewall Manager he opens the WAF page, protection packs, and picks a category. He chooses content and publishing, web rather than API, and selects a CloudFront distribution he already has.
  • The wizard would attach a web ACL. He stops, because he says it would add cost to that distribution for no benefit in this course.
  • The lab is the protection-pack form, not a saved firewall.
  • He is consistent with the CloudFront lecture: WAF is optional and it is not free.

304 Network Firewall and Firewall Manager

  • Network Firewall is a managed firewall for a VPC: stateful and stateless rules, domain filtering, and intrusion detection and prevention.
  • Stateful, in his words, tracks the connection. Stateless matches the packet in front of it.
  • Firewall Manager pushes rules across accounts and organizations: WAF rules onto new CloudFront distributions, and security-group standards onto VPCs.
  • One is the firewall. The other is how a company applies firewalls everywhere.

305 KMS and CloudHSM

  • KMS creates and stores encryption keys and works with S3, EBS, RDS, and Lambda. You manage a key policy. AWS runs the hardware.
  • CloudHSM is a hardware security module in your VPC when a rule says you must control the key material yourself. He cites FIPS 140-2 level 3.
  • Use KMS unless a regulation or a custom cryptographic application forces CloudHSM.
  • Keys in KMS can encrypt the volumes and buckets from the earlier units.

306 Hands-on: KMS

  • Create key, symmetric, encrypt and decrypt. He says asymmetric is a public and private pair. Alias such as random demo key.
  • He walks the key administrators and key users pages and finishes the create.
  • Keys are not deleted immediately. He schedules deletion with the seven-day minimum and the key shows pending deletion.
  • He reads the warning that data encrypted under the key will not be recoverable.

307 Certificate Manager

  • This is the same ACM story as lecture 044, now under the right name: provision and renew SSL/TLS certificates.
  • Public certificates come from Amazon’s public certificate authority for names you control. Private certificates are for internal apps.
  • He attaches them in his explanation to load balancers, CloudFront, and API Gateway.
  • You stop buying and renewing the certificate by hand.

308 Secrets Manager

  • Secrets Manager stores database passwords, API keys, and tokens.
  • It can rotate them and keep the previous value as a version.
  • Applications read the secret at runtime instead of baking the password into the code.
  • He keeps this lecture short. The console is next.

309 Hands-on: Secrets Manager

  • Store a new secret. He shows the types: RDS, DocumentDB, Redshift, another database, or a generic key.
  • For a database secret he would type a username and password and pick a KMS key. He has no database selected in the first pass, so he also shows the other-secret path.
  • The recording includes deleting a database instance he had created for the secret, with “delete me” and no final snapshot.
  • Delete the secret when the demo is over. Rotation is explained, not left running against a live database.

310 Artifact

  • Artifact is the library of AWS compliance reports: SOC, ISO, and the agreements you sign, such as a BAA.
  • Exam phrase he wants: compliance documents in one place means Artifact.
  • You download reports for an auditor. You do not configure a firewall here.
  • Uses: internal audit, a regulator, and a customer asking how AWS is certified.

311 GuardDuty

  • GuardDuty watches the account for suspicious activity using machine learning and threat feeds. He names VPC flow logs, DNS logs, CloudTrail, and EKS audit logs as inputs.
  • Findings include port scans, odd API calls, and credential misuse.
  • It does not block the call. It reports it.
  • He is clear it is a detector, not the place you write a firewall rule.

312 Inspector

  • Inspector scans EC2 instances, ECR images, and Lambda functions for software vulnerabilities and network exposure.
  • A finding is an unpatched package or an exposure, not a person attacking you right now. That live suspicion is GuardDuty.
  • ECR scanning runs on images you push.
  • You patch what it reports. It does not patch for you.

313 AWS Config

  • Config records how resources are configured and how that configuration changes.
  • Rules check the history against a standard, such as “S3 buckets are encrypted” or “security groups did not open a port.”
  • It aggregates across regions. He says the exam phrase is tracking configuration changes.
  • CloudTrail says who called the API. Config says what the resource looked like before and after.

314 Macie

  • Macie uses machine learning to find sensitive data, especially personally identifiable information, in S3.
  • Examples he gives: a home address, a bank account number, a password sitting in a bucket.
  • It classifies the data and alerts you. He says S3 is where it is used most, even if it can be pointed more widely.
  • The job is discovery and privacy, not encryption. Encryption was KMS.

315 Detective

  • Detective investigates a finding. GuardDuty or a similar alert says something happened. Detective tries to show the root cause and the related activity over time.
  • He includes failed logins, policy changes, and privilege changes in what it can visualize.
  • It is a graph of behavior, not another scanner.
  • You open it after you already have a suspicion.

316 AWS abuse

  • Abuse is using AWS against the acceptable-use rules: phishing sites, running DDoS attacks, distributing malware, or mining cryptocurrency from compromised accounts.
  • Phishing, in his definition, is a fake site that steals credentials.
  • You report it through the abuse channel. This lecture is not a how-to for any of those activities.
  • He lists port scanning and brute force as further examples of what gets reported.

317 Root user privileges

  • Some actions are root-only: close the account, change billing, see tax invoices, and he also lists creating IAM users, enabling or disabling MFA, and changing service-control policies.
  • He adds root-level support cases, Artifact agreements, key rotation in some cases, and turning on services such as Macie and Control Tower.
  • Daily admin work should be an IAM user with only the permissions that job needs.
  • Lock the root credentials away and use them when the action cannot be done any other way. MFA on root is one of the four habits he says to keep.

318 Shared responsibility

  • The recording is the EBS, EFS, and instance-store split again.
  • AWS replaces hardware and runs replication and the encryption implementation. You tick encryption, you schedule snapshots, and you own the bytes.
  • Instance store disappears when the instance stops.
  • It is not a new security-service model. The security split he has been using all along is: AWS runs the tool, you turn it on and you read the findings.

What he is doing in this unit

Running a Shield network analysis against a small instance, opening the WAF protection-pack wizard and backing out, creating a KMS key and scheduling its deletion, and storing then removing a secret. The other services are identification lectures.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Separate Shield Standard, Shield Advanced, and WAF by what they see.
  • Create a symmetric KMS key and schedule deletion with a waiting period.
  • Match GuardDuty, Inspector, Config, Macie, and Detective to “threat,” “vulnerability,” “configuration,” “sensitive data,” and “investigation.”
  • List actions he reserves for the root user, and leave root locked.

32 — Management and billing

319 Management and billing overview

  • First the multi-account tools: Organizations, service control policies, consolidated billing, Control Tower, and Resource Access Manager.
  • Then the money tools: Cost Explorer, Budgets, Cost Anomaly Detection, and Trusted Advisor. He also names Compute Optimizer in the overview.
  • The hands-on is one pass through the billing console, including a zero-spend budget.
  • This is the unit that explains the bills he has been warning you about.

320 AWS Organizations

  • Organizations groups many accounts under one management account. Accounts sit in organizational units such as dev and production.
  • Consolidated billing is one payment. He still wants the accounts separate so a developer cannot change production.
  • Delegated admins let one member account run a particular service for the organization. APIs can create accounts instead of clicking through signup each time.
  • Service control policies, next lecture, are how the organization says what an account may not do.

321 Service control policies

  • An SCP does not grant permission. It sets the maximum. If the SCP does not allow an action, IAM cannot allow it, not even for root of that member account.
  • His example policy denies ec2:TerminateInstances and s3:DeleteBucket on all resources. The JSON shape matches the IAM policies from earlier: Version, Statement, Effect, Action, Resource.
  • SCPs attach to member accounts and organizational units, not to the management account. A new account starts with full AWS access until you attach a tighter SCP. You still need IAM inside the account.
  • Inheritance: a deny on the production unit blocks Redshift for every account under it, even if a lower policy looks more open. A deny on the HR unit adds Lambda to that block. The finance unit has no SCP of its own, so it only inherits the production deny.

322 Consolidated billing

  • One management account pays for every member account.
  • Usage is added together, so volume discounts apply to the total. You can still see each account’s share.
  • There is no extra fee for the single bill, and combining the bill does not combine the permissions.
  • It is a billing feature of Organizations, not a new account type.

323 Control Tower

  • Control Tower sets up a multi-account landing zone with guardrails, which are pre-built rules for security and operations.
  • Account Factory is the self-service way to vend a new account from a blueprint. A dashboard shows which guardrails each account meets.
  • He integrates it with IAM, SCPs, CloudTrail, Config, and SSO.
  • Exam line he uses: “best practices,” guardrails, and Account Factory mean Control Tower. “Group the accounts” without that means Organizations.

324 Resource Access Manager

  • RAM shares a resource with another account, an organizational unit, or the whole organization, without copying it.
  • He lists VPCs, transit gateways, Route 53 resolver rules, license configurations, and Aurora clusters.
  • You pay for the resource, not for the share.
  • The other account uses the resource in place. You do not export a copy.

325 Cost Explorer

  • Cost Explorer graphs spend by time, by service, by account, and by tag, and it forecasts if you keep the current pace.
  • He uses it to see which service dominates, then to set a budget from that forecast.
  • Tags such as project or environment are how one graph splits a shared account.
  • The tool is free. The lesson is to look before the invoice is a surprise.

326 Budgets

  • A budget is a number you set for cost, usage, or for Savings Plans and Reserved Instance coverage.
  • Alerts go by email or SNS at thresholds he examples as 80 percent and 100 percent.
  • Filters use tags, so a team can have its own budget. Forecast alerts warn you before the month ends.
  • Cost Explorer explains the past. Budgets watch the present against a limit.

327 Cost Anomaly Detection

  • This watches for spend that does not look like your normal pattern, using machine learning, and it notifies you.
  • His example is an Auto Scaling Group multiplying instances during a traffic spike and the bill jumping the same day.
  • You scope a monitor by service, account, or cost category. The report includes region and the size of the surprise.
  • It evaluates on a daily or weekly cadence. It does not stop the resource. It tells you.

328 Hands-on: billing console

  • He opens Billing and Cost Management: month-to-date, forecast, last month (he mentions a prior total around $14), and a budget already breached. His example budget was $1 and the actual was several times that.
  • Cost Explorer: group by service and EC2 is the largest bar, then by region (Mumbai and N. Virginia in his account), then by instance type. He changes the date range and shows a daily view. Hourly granularity has no data because he has not enabled it.
  • He shows cost and usage reports as a CSV download, the anomaly-detection monitor form (he does not create one), and budget templates.
  • He creates a zero-spend budget: alert above $0.01, his email, create. He recommends that template for a student account.

329 Trusted Advisor

  • Trusted Advisor checks the account against best practices in cost, performance, security, fault tolerance, and service limits.
  • Cost checks include idle or underused resources and a cheaper purchase option. Limit checks warn when you approach a quota, such as running out of free-tier hours.
  • He describes it as recommendations, not as a changer. You still decide to stop the instance.
  • This is the last service lecture in the course.

What he is doing in this unit

Reading his own bill in Cost Explorer by service, region, and instance type, and creating a zero-spend budget that emails him above one cent. Organizations, SCPs, Control Tower, and RAM are diagrams and policies, not a second account he creates on camera.

After this unit you should be able to

  • Describe an organizational unit and say an SCP only denies, it does not grant.
  • Tell Control Tower (landing zone and guardrails) from Organizations (the account tree).
  • Open Cost Explorer and group spend by service.
  • Create a zero-spend budget with an email alert.
  • Say what Trusted Advisor is for: advice across cost, security, and limits.
